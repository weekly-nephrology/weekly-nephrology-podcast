Our second case is a young man with protein in his urine. CEN Case Reports, again abstract only.

Oligomeganephronia is a rare congenital condition in which the kidneys form with far too few nephrons, and the ones that are there grow large to compensate. It is normally picked up in childhood, so meeting it for the first time in an adult is unusual, and the authors describe it as something that often goes unrecognised.

Hold on to that word. The story turns on how he was born.

The patient was a man in his late twenties who had been born extremely preterm, at twenty eight weeks, as one of triplets. Protein had been turning up in his urine on and off for years without anyone chasing it down, and by the time he was referred he was training regularly with weights and taking protein supplements.

(Exact values: 29-year-old man; Born at 28 weeks of gestation as one of triplets; Birth weight 740 g; Proteinuria intermittently detected for 10 years before investigation.)

That's the presentation. The numbers are where it gets strange.

Three months before he was admitted, his urine protein to creatinine ratio had climbed to about one point two grams per gram. His serum creatinine at that point was around one and a half milligrams per decilitre. What makes the picture interesting is that his kidney function measures did not agree with each other, because the estimate from creatinine and the estimate from cystatin C pointed in different directions, while a directly measured inulin clearance was actually preserved, and his urinary beta two microglobulin was only mildly raised.

(Exact values: Urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio 1.20 g/gCr, three months before admission; Serum creatinine 1.54 mg/dL at the same timepoint; Urinary β2-microglobulin mildly elevated on admission; creatinine-based and cystatin C-based eGFR discrepant; inulin clearance preserved.)

Now the biopsy, where the pieces line up.

The kidney biopsy showed glomeruli that were strikingly large and spread thinly, meaning there were fewer of them packed into the tissue than you would expect, together with a perihilar pattern of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. Read alongside his birth history, the team took that as oligomeganephronia with damage added on top from hyperfiltration.

He was started on losartan, and the abstract says the protein in his urine came down quickly. Over the following nine months his kidney function stayed stable.

The third case is a biopsy that refuses to be one disease. Also CEN Case Reports, abstract only.

ANCA associated vasculitis is classically a pauci immune disease, which means the biopsy shows crescents but very little immune deposit. Immune complexes are being recognised in a subset of patients though, and what that actually means clinically is still not settled. An overlap with membranous nephropathy like changes on top of that is described as uncommon, and as something that can muddy both the diagnosis and the treatment.

That's the tension. Here's the patient.

The patient was a woman in her thirties whose only clue was abnormal urine on a routine health check. Her protein loss was a little under two grams per gram of creatinine, and her estimated filtration rate had slipped to around fifty, with plenty of red cells in the sediment as well.

(Exact values: 37-year-old woman; Proteinuria 1.83 g/gCr; Microscopic hematuria > 50 red blood cells per high-power field; eGFR 51.4 mL/min/1.73 m2.)

Then the blood work and the biopsy, side by side.

Her MPO ANCA was raised, and her complement levels were not low. The biopsy showed a crescentic glomerulonephritis, which fits the ANCA story, but there was also granular staining for IgG, C3 and C1q in the mesangium and along the capillary walls, and electron microscopy found deposits on the outer side of the basement membrane, which is the pattern you associate with membranous nephropathy.

And here's the part I'd slow down for.

The antigen work is where it gets interesting. The subclass staining picked up IgG one through three but not IgG four, and the usual primary membranous targets, PLA2R, THSD7A and NELL1, were all negative. What did light up was EXT1, with EXT2 negative, and taking everything together the authors said this profile was not what you see in a primary membranous nephropathy.

Then the finding that reframed the case.

Looking further, they found anti SSA antibodies and eye findings that fitted Sjögren's syndrome. She was then treated with high dose corticosteroids, rituximab and avacopan, and both her kidney function and her urine findings gradually improved, though the protein was slow to come down compared with everything else.

And the limits, which the authors are unusually plain about.

On the limits, the authors are explicit that they could not pin the membranous part on one single disease process, and they leave it as overlapping autoimmune mechanisms rather than a proven mechanism. We also only have the abstract, so there is no follow up length, no final protein or kidney function figure, and no repeat biopsy, and with three drugs given together a single case cannot tell us which one did the work.

So this one ends unresolved, and that's not a failure of the report.

Now to the case we'll spend most time on, and the only one where I read the whole paper. Open access, in CEN Case Reports.

Start with the phenomenon, because the name does a lot of work.

Tumor to tumor metastasis is the odd situation where cancer from one site travels and then settles inside a completely separate tumor. Pathologists only accept the label when they can show two genuinely different tumors growing together, with the visitor invading the host tissue rather than sitting in a vessel or simply lying beside it. Kidney cancer is a frequent host, and yet spread from a breast cancer into a kidney cancer is rare enough that the authors could gather only eleven published reports from the past two decades, with their own patient adding one more.

(Exact values: 11 cases of breast carcinoma metastasizing to renal cell carcinoma reported worldwide since 2006; 12 cases including the present case, summarized in Table 1; A cited systematic review identified 28 breast-to-kidney tumor-to-tumor metastasis events and more than 600 documented tumor-to-tumor metastasis events overall.)

Now the patient.

The patient was a Japanese woman in her sixties who had been living with hormone receptor positive breast cancer for well over a decade, including bone metastases treated through a long run of endocrine therapies. She was on fulvestrant with capivasertib when a routine follow up scan picked up a single, sharply outlined mass of about twenty three millimetres in her right kidney. It took up contrast strongly and stayed bright on the later images, and apart from bone, nothing else looked abnormal at that point.

(Exact values: 64-year-old Japanese woman; Invasive ductal carcinoma of the left breast diagnosed in 2007; ER Allred 5 + 2, PgR Allred 4 + 2, HER2 IHC score 2+; Bone metastases developed in 2011; Solitary 23-mm right renal mass on contrast-enhanced CT; lesion detected in 2025 during systemic therapy with fulvestrant and capivasertib.)

Hold that picture in your head. Now, the surgery and the slides.

Because the mass looked like a straightforward clear cell kidney cancer, the team skipped a needle biopsy and went ahead with a robot assisted partial nephrectomy, which passed off without complications. On the slides, most of the tumor really was clear cell renal cell carcinoma, but sitting inside it were separate nests of breast cancer that were invading the surrounding tissue, and on low power those nests took up roughly a third of the tumor area. The margins came back clear, kidney function stayed steady afterwards, and she was seen at the hospital for about a month before oncology follow up moved back to her referring hospital.

(Exact values: Metastatic breast carcinoma accounted for approximately 30% of the tumor area on low-power examination; Clear cell RCC component: pT1a, WHO/ISUP grade 1 with focal grade 2 nuclear features, no capsular invasion, negative surgical margins; Follow-up at the treating institution: 1 month, with no surgical complications.)

Which raises the obvious question. How do you prove those nests came from the breast?

The immunostaining came out almost as a mirror image, and that is what settled the diagnosis. The kidney cancer cells carried PAX8 and CA9 but none of the breast markers, while the intruding nests were positive for CK7 and GATA3, with weak estrogen receptor staining and no kidney markers at all. That weak estrogen receptor signal matched the receptor status of her original breast tumor, and because the deposits were pushing into the surrounding tissue instead of sitting inside vessels, the authors could argue this was true growth of one tumor inside another rather than two tumors meeting or cells trapped in a vessel.

(Exact values: RCC component: PAX8-positive, CA9-positive, CK7-negative, GATA3-negative, hormone receptor-negative; Metastatic breast carcinoma component: CK7-positive, GATA3-positive, weak ER expression, PAX8-negative, CA9-negative; Primary breast carcinoma reference: ER Allred 5 + 2; metastatic component ER-positive but with lower staining intensity.)

That's the pivot of the case. Now, what the authors make of it.

The message the authors take away is that a clear cell kidney cancer can act as a host for breast cancer deposits, and that in this patient the scan gave no hint at all, because the mass looked entirely typical. They also explain why the label matters. Calling it plain kidney cancer could distort the staging or be misread as one tumor simply behaving strangely, whereas here the finding counted as evidence that the breast cancer had progressed, and the team planned to move her onto a different systemic drug regimen.

And the limits.

There are real limits worth saying out loud. This is a single patient, and the team only followed her briefly before her care moved back to the referring hospital, so we learn nothing about the longer course. The authors also point out that a small metastatic focus hiding inside a dominant kidney tumor can be missed when only part of the tissue is sampled, that many of the older published cases came before routine immunostaining and so are less certain, and that they cannot say how much of breast cancer's frequent appearance as a donor reflects biology rather than simply how common and long lived that cancer is.

And one thing covers all of today's stories. A case report is a single patient, written up once. It shows something is possible. It can't tell you how often, and it can't prove a treatment worked.
