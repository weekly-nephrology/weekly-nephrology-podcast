This one is in Nature Medicine, published at the end of August, from Brigham and Women's Hospital and Harvard Medical School in Boston.

Researchers pooled individual participant data from just under thirty-nine thousand adults with heart failure who had taken part in nine randomized trials, about seventeen thousand of them with reduced ejection fraction and about twenty-two thousand with mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction, and they built a model to estimate what an ARNI, a steroidal or nonsteroidal MRA, and an SGLT2 inhibitor, alone or in combination, do to blood pressure, eGFR and serum potassium over the first two to twelve weeks of treatment.

(Exact values: 38,753 participants across nine randomized trials; 16,877 with HFrEF; 21,876 with HFmrEF/HFpEF; short-term window: 2-12 weeks.)

So, a model built on trial data, not a fresh trial.

For trial participants with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, the model estimated that the combination of an ARNI, an SGLT2 inhibitor and a steroidal MRA lowers systolic blood pressure by about nine millimetres of mercury within the first two to twelve weeks, with a ninety-five percent prediction interval running from about eight to about eleven millimetres of mercury.

(Exact values: HFrEF, ARNI + SGLT2i + sMRA, SBP: -9.2 mmHg (95% PI -10.6 to -7.8).)

(Exact values: HFrEF, ARNI + SGLT2i + sMRA, eGFR: -6.8 ml min-1 m-2 (95% PI -7.9 to -5.4); HFrEF, ARNI + SGLT2i + sMRA, serum potassium: +0.30 mmol l-1 (95% PI 0.25 to 0.35).)

And that's the pairing that makes people hesitate at the prescribing screen.

This was a cross-sectional study of just over five thousand adults with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease who had been referred for a kidney workup that included isotopic measured GFR rather than an estimated value, and hyperkalemia was defined as a plasma potassium of five millimoles per litre or more, or the use of a potassium binder.

(Exact values: 5046 adults with non-dialysis CKD; hyperkalemia definition: plasma potassium >=5.0 mmol/L or potassium binder use.)

Note the measured part. That's unusual, and it's why this cohort is worth the time.

Overall, about six percent of this referred non-dialysis CKD population met the hyperkalemia definition, but the gradient across kidney function was steep: around one percent in CKD stages one and two, eight percent in stage three, and twenty-one percent in stages four and five.

(Exact values: overall hyperkalemia prevalence 6.4%; CKD stages 1-2: 1%; CKD stage 3: 8%; CKD stages 4-5: 21%.)

The gradient is the thing to carry away here.

In the multivariable model covering these five thousand adults with non-dialysis CKD, each ten millilitre per minute drop in measured GFR was associated with about a sixty-five percent higher odds of hyperkalemia, and that association was stronger among people taking a RAAS inhibitor, with a test for interaction that reached statistical significance; diabetes, RAAS inhibitors, calcineurin inhibitors, a higher urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio and a lower venous bicarbonate were also independently associated with hyperkalemia in this cross-sectional cohort.

Now the caution, and it's structural.

Because the design is cross-sectional, potassium and its associated factors were measured at the same moment, so these are associations rather than causes, and the cohort is a specialist referral population in which more than a third were kidney transplant recipients; the abstract also reports no clinical consequences such as arrhythmia, hospitalization or death.

(Exact values: 5046 adults in a cross-sectional referral cohort; 36% kidney transplant recipients; 0 clinical outcomes (arrhythmia, hospitalization, death) reported in the abstract.)

Next, a trial in a group that usually gets left out of trials.

It's in the Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, published in early September, from Renji Hospital at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine.

This was a multicentre, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in dialysis patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, who were assigned one to one to henagliflozin, an SGLT2 inhibitor, or to placebo for twenty-four weeks on top of their usual therapy, with the primary outcome being the change in left ventricular mass index at twenty-four weeks and secondary outcomes covering left atrial volume index, E over e prime, e prime, NT-proBNP and safety.

(Exact values: randomized 1:1 to henagliflozin or placebo; treatment duration 24 weeks; primary outcome: change in LVMI from baseline to week 24.)

An imaging endpoint, then. Worth marking before the result.

One hundred and twelve dialysis patients with preserved ejection fraction heart failure were randomized, fifty-six to henagliflozin and fifty-six to placebo, and the group was split roughly evenly between hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis, with sixty on hemodialysis and fifty-two on peritoneal dialysis.

(Exact values: 112 randomized: henagliflozin n=56, placebo n=56; 60 on hemodialysis, 52 on peritoneal dialysis.)

Small, but for this population that's more than we usually have.

Over twenty-four weeks in these dialysis patients with preserved ejection fraction heart failure, left ventricular mass index fell slightly in the henagliflozin group and rose slightly on placebo, leaving a between-group difference of about five grams per square metre in favour of henagliflozin, with a confidence interval that ran from just half a gram to about ten grams and a p value of zero point zero three.

(Exact values: LVMI least-squares mean change: -3.27 +/- 1.71 g/m2 (henagliflozin) vs 2.01 +/- 1.69 g/m2 (placebo); between-group difference -5.27 g/m2 (95% CI -10.02 to -0.53), P=0.03.)

Which raises the obvious question. Did anything else move with it?

In this dialysis HFpEF trial, none of the secondary outcomes, including left atrial volume index, E over e prime, e prime and NT-proBNP, showed a between-group difference, and adverse events occurred in a similar proportion of each group, thirty-six percent with henagliflozin and thirty-four percent with placebo.

(Exact values: adverse events: 36% (henagliflozin) vs 34% (placebo).)

And here's where the authors are honest about what they've shown.

The trial is small, with one hundred and twelve dialysis patients followed for twenty-four weeks, and its endpoint is an imaging measure rather than how patients felt or fared, so the authors themselves say larger studies are needed to confirm the effect on left ventricular mass, and the abstract reports no hospitalizations, symptoms or deaths.

(Exact values: 112 randomized participants; follow-up 24 weeks; 0 clinical outcomes (hospitalization, symptoms, death) reported in the abstract.)

And here's the part that earned it a place today.

Regression. That's a heavy word, so let's be careful with it.

Our interest here isn't the coronary result. It's the safety endpoint, so that's where I'll spend the time.

For a kidney audience the key caveat is that contrast-associated acute kidney injury only appears inside a composite with major bleeding, so the abstract does not tell us how many kidney injury events there were on their own, how much contrast each strategy used, or what the baseline kidney function of these patients was.

One more, and it steps back from any single result.

This is in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation, published at the end of August, from the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens.

This study did not enrol patients; it studied the trials themselves, looking at randomized trials of SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists and nonsteroidal MRAs in adults with chronic kidney disease, and it measured something the authors call the enrollment disparity difference, which is simply the share of women actually enrolled minus the share you would expect from global estimates of how common CKD is in women with matching characteristics.

(Exact values: 3 drug classes assessed: SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists and nonsteroidal MRAs; enrollment disparity difference = observed proportion of female participants minus expected proportion (1 benchmark: sex-specific Global Burden of Disease CKD prevalence estimates).)

A study of studies, then, rather than of patients.

Across the fifty-five randomized CKD trials that were analysed, the median share of women enrolled was about a third, and almost ninety-five percent of the trials enrolled fewer women than the disease burden would predict.

(Exact values: 55 randomized trials included; median female enrollment 33.1%; 52 of 55 trials (94.5%) enrolled fewer women than expected.)

That's a blunt way to open a paper.

Pooling the fifty-five randomized CKD trials, women were under-enrolled by about seventeen percentage points compared with what CKD burden would predict, with a ninety-five percent confidence interval of roughly fourteen to twenty points, and the shortfall pointed the same way for every drug class examined.

(Exact values: pooled enrollment disparity difference -0.17 (95% CI -0.20 to -0.14).)

And before anyone turns a paper like this into a head-to-head argument, the limits.

Two caveats matter here: the whole analysis sits at the level of published trials rather than individual patients, and the size of the shortfall depends entirely on the Global Burden of Disease prevalence estimates chosen as the benchmark, so the factors linked to under-enrolment are associations across trials rather than demonstrated causes.
