Here's a question. When a new patient sits down in front of you, how much attention do you give to their teeth?

Probably not much. Teeth feel like somebody else's department.

WDR72 is a gene best known in dentistry, because damaging variants in it produce hypoplastic amelogenesis imperfecta, the condition where tooth enamel forms badly. More recently it has also been tied to distal renal tubular acidosis. So the same gene can show up in the enamel and in the part of the nephron that handles acid.

So much for teeth being someone else's problem.

Now, where today's material comes from. Three of the four cases are abstracts, and the fourth we read as verified open-access full text.

An abstract gives you the outline of the reasoning and very little else, so what I can say about those three has to stay modest.

The full-text case is where we can follow the whole timeline, and hear what the authors were unsure about. Three quick looks, then one long one.

Now, let's get started.

Our first case is a report in CEN Case Reports, published this August, from a paediatric nephrology unit in Chandigarh, in India. Abstract only.

Here's how it presented.

The patient was a twelve-year-old boy, and what brought him in was trouble walking, along with deformities of his lower limbs and growth that had fallen well behind. On examination he had poorly formed tooth enamel, rickets and short stature, so the picture was already pointing at something systemic rather than a local bone problem.

(Exact values: 12-year-old boy: difficulty walking, lower limb deformities, significant growth retardation; hypoplastic amelogenesis imperfecta, rickets and short stature on examination.)

Now to the tests.

The laboratory picture in this twelve-year-old boy pulled the diagnosis together: potassium was low, phosphate was low, alkaline phosphatase was raised, and he was losing too much calcium in the urine. Imaging then added two things — calcium deposited in the medulla of the kidney, and cysts. The abstract describes the pattern rather than the individual numbers, so the actual values are not reported.

(Exact values: 12-year-old boy: hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, elevated alkaline phosphatase and hypercalciuria; medullary nephrocalcinosis and renal cysts on imaging (exact laboratory values not_reported in the abstract).)

Next, the genetics.

Genetic testing closed the loop. Clinical exome sequencing in this twelve-year-old boy found a pathogenic nonsense variant in WDR72 present on both copies of the gene, and that fits an autosomal recessive pattern. With the enamel findings, the acidosis and the genetics lined up, he was diagnosed with distal renal tubular acidosis.

(Exact values: WDR72 (NM_182758.4) c.2934G>A, p.Trp978Ter: homozygous pathogenic nonsense variant, autosomal recessive.)

Then treatment, and the follow-up.

Treatment was potassium citrate together with phosphate supplements, and the follow-up ran about two years. Over that time this twelve-year-old boy grew faster than he had been growing and his urinary calcium came down. What did not change is the part that worries you: the calcium deposits in the kidney and the cysts were still there at the end of the two years.

(Exact values: potassium citrate plus phosphate supplementation, followed over 2 years; at 2 years: improved growth velocity and reduced hypercalciuria; medullary nephrocalcinosis and renal cysts persisted.)

Before we move on, what this one can and can't tell us.

Two cautions belong with this one. We only have the abstract, and it describes a single child, so whether cysts are truly part of this genetic condition or simply happened to be present in this boy is not something one patient can settle. The authors also suggest that a long delay before diagnosis may leave structural damage that treatment cannot undo, and that is their reading of one clinical course, not a proven sequence of events.

(Exact values: evidence base: 1 patient, abstract only; the persistence of cysts and nephrocalcinosis is described in this single 12-year-old boy.)

Our second case is a report in Kidney Medicine, published in June, from the clinical nephrology service at the National Institutes of Health in Bethesda. Abstract only again.

Start with the name, because it's an acronym, and the letters do the work.

ROSAH is an acronym, and every letter stands for one feature: R for retinal dystrophy, O for optic nerve edema, meaning swelling of the optic nerve, S for splenomegaly, an enlarged spleen, A for anhidrosis, an inability to sweat, and H for headache. It is a rare autoinflammatory disorder passed on in an autosomal dominant way, and it comes from gain-of-function mutations in a gene called ALPK1. Those mutations switch on NF-kappa-B signalling, which is why the illness spreads across so many organ systems rather than staying in one place.

That's the syndrome. Now the patient.

The patient in this report is a forty-four-year-old man, and for a while his kidney disease had a different name: he carried a diagnosis of lupus nephritis before ROSAH syndrome was recognised. What he actually presented with was mild chronic kidney disease along with proteinuria that stayed below the nephrotic range. The abstract does not give his kidney function or protein numbers, so those are not reported.

(Exact values: 44-year-old man; mild chronic kidney disease with subnephrotic proteinuria (eGFR and protein quantification not_reported in the abstract).)

And here's the biopsy.

This is the part that explains the earlier label. The biopsy in this forty-four-year-old man showed an immune complex glomerulonephritis with only minimal mesangial proliferation, and that appearance looks very much like class two lupus nephritis. What did not fit was everything around it: he had neither the clinical features nor the blood markers of systemic lupus erythematosus.

(Exact values: 44-year-old man; kidney biopsy: immune complex-mediated glomerulonephritis with minimal mesangial proliferation; appearance resembling class II lupus nephritis; no clinical or serological markers of systemic lupus erythematosus.)

Which leaves the obvious question. How often does this actually happen?

It is worth being clear about what this can and cannot show. We have the abstract only, so the kidney findings come from one patient, and the cohort analysis is mentioned without its results. A single man whose biopsy mimicked class two lupus nephritis tells us that this mimic exists and is worth remembering; it does not tell us how often ROSAH sits behind a diagnosis of lupus nephritis, and the abstract gives us no treatment or follow-up for him either.

(Exact values: evidence base: 1 patient with biopsy findings, abstract only; cohort prevalence results not_reported.)

Our third case is another report in CEN Case Reports, also published this August, from a nephrology department in Osaka. Abstract only.

Start with the disease itself.

Anti-GBM disease is the one where antibodies go after the glomerular basement membrane, and the classic picture is a rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, sometimes with bleeding into the lungs as well. What the authors emphasise is that the brain is almost never part of the story. Involvement of the central nervous system is exceedingly uncommon in this disease, and bleeding into the subarachnoid space has only rarely been described alongside it.

Now the patient, and how she arrived.

The patient was a fifty-three-year-old woman, and she arrived with symptoms that could have been almost anything: fever and headache. Her kidneys then failed quickly, and her anti-GBM antibody titre came back markedly raised. A kidney biopsy confirmed the diagnosis. The abstract describes the titre as markedly elevated without giving the actual figure, so the number itself is not reported.

(Exact values: 53-year-old woman: fever and headache at presentation, rapid acute kidney injury, markedly elevated anti-GBM antibody titers (numeric titers not_reported in the abstract), biopsy-confirmed anti-GBM disease.)

Treatment next.

In this fifty-three-year-old woman, treatment went the way we all fear in this disease. She was given plasma exchange and high-dose corticosteroids, and even so her kidney function kept deteriorating until she was dependent on dialysis. Because the response to conventional treatment was not enough to bring the antibody under control, rituximab was added on top.

And then the course changed.

Then came the turn nobody expects. During her hospital course this fifty-three-year-old woman had a generalised seizure, and imaging showed a subarachnoid haemorrhage inside the skull; later she also had a subacute cerebral infarction. Cerebral angiography looked for the usual culprits and found none — no aneurysm, no vascular malformation — and her blood pressure had been stable in the run-up to the neurological event.

(Exact values: 1 generalized seizure followed by intracranial subarachnoid hemorrhage and a later subacute cerebral infarction in a 53-year-old woman; cerebral angiography showed no aneurysms or vascular malformations; blood pressure stable before the event.)

So where did all of that leave her?

The outcome splits in two. On the neurological side, with continued plasma exchange, rituximab, corticosteroids and tight blood pressure control, this fifty-three-year-old woman came through without any lasting deficits, and her anti-GBM antibody titres came down gradually. On the kidney side, nothing was recovered: her kidney function did not come back.

Before we leave this one, the limits again.

The limits here are worth stating plainly. This is one patient, and we have only the abstract. The bleed happened during the course of her anti-GBM disease and angiography found no structural cause, but one case cannot tell us that the disease or its treatment produced the haemorrhage. The authors present it as a rare complication that deserves attention on the neurological side, not as a mechanism they have proven.

(Exact values: evidence base: 1 patient, abstract only; no mechanism and no comparison group reported.)

Our last case is the one we read in full. It's in Kidney Medicine, published in July, from the department of nephrology at the University of Tsukuba, in Japan.

Because we have the whole paper, we can take this one slowly.

First, the background you need.

Polycythemia vera is a blood cancer that starts in the bone marrow stem cells, and in most patients it is driven by a gain-of-function change in the JAK2 gene, most often the V617F variant, which leaves the JAK-STAT signalling pathway switched on. Kidney trouble in polycythemia vera is usually about blood flow or clotting, but glomerular disease turns up too. When the authors searched the literature, they found roughly eighteen earlier reports of polycythemia vera together with biopsy-proven IgA nephropathy, and as far as they can tell, not one of those patients had been given a JAK inhibitor.

(Exact values: JAK2 V617F: somatic gain-of-function mutation present in the majority of patients with polycythemia vera; approximately 18 previously reported cases of polycythemia vera complicated by biopsy-proven IgA nephropathy (Table 1); Table 1 lists 19 rows in total, 18 prior cases plus the present case.)

Now the patient, and how her story unfolded.

The timeline matters here. In this 73-year-old woman, blood in the urine turned up about a year after her polycythemia vera was diagnosed, and roughly a year after that protein appeared as well. Then things moved quickly: over a couple of months her creatinine went from around zero point eight five up to one point one, and it peaked at about one point four, while her urine protein-to-creatinine ratio peaked near two point seven grams per gram. The autoimmune workup was reassuring in a way that made the picture harder — antinuclear antibody, ANCA and anti-GBM antibody were all negative, and her complement and immunoglobulin levels sat in the normal range.

(Exact values: serum creatinine 0.85 mg/dL when proteinuria first appeared, 1.11 mg/dL two months later, peak 1.39 mg/dL; peak urinary protein-creatinine ratio 2.7 g/g creatinine; urinalysis progression: occult blood 1+ then 2+, proteinuria 2+ then 3+; antinuclear antibody, ANCA and anti-GBM antibody negative; C3, C4 and immunoglobulins within normal range; age: 73-year-old woman in the Abstract and Case Report; Table 1 lists the present case with age at biopsy 75 years (unexplained discrepancy inside the source).)

Next, the biopsy.

The biopsy is where the diagnosis landed. The sample from this 73-year-old woman held twelve glomeruli, one of them completely scarred, which left eleven that could actually be judged. Those showed patchy mesangial proliferation, two glomeruli with cellular crescents, one with a fibrous crescent and one with segmental scarring, and there was patchy tubular atrophy with moderate inflammation in the interstitium. Immunofluorescence showed granular IgA in the mesangium with only a trace of C3c and nothing else lighting up. The diagnosis was IgA nephropathy with cellular crescents, and the Oxford score came out as M zero, S one, E zero, T zero, C one.

(Exact values: 12 glomeruli sampled, 1 globally sclerosed (11 assessable); 2 glomeruli with cellular crescents, 1 with a fibrous crescent, 1 with a segmental sclerotic lesion; immunofluorescence: IgA 1+ granular mesangial, C3c trace; IgG, IgM, C1q and C4 negative; Oxford classification M0S1E0T0C1.)

Then the treatment decision. This is the part I'd most want you to hear.

Here is the fork in the road. On biopsy grounds steroids were the obvious next step, but her white cell count was already high from the polycythemia vera, and the team worried that steroids would push it higher and blur their reading of the blood disorder, so they treated the blood disorder first with the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib. Over the following year this 73-year-old woman's urinary protein fell below half a gram a day, her white cell count settled near nine thousand, her hemoglobin sat around eleven, and her creatinine levelled off at roughly one point five.

(Exact values: ruxolitinib: urinary protein reduced to <0.5 g/d within 1 year; white blood cell count stabilized at approximately 9,000/microlitre; hemoglobin approximately 11 g/dL; serum creatinine stabilized at approximately 1.5 mg/dL.)

The story doesn't end there.

The urinary findings did not go away completely, though, and with cellular crescents on the biopsy that mattered. So a year after the biopsy prednisolone was added at thirty milligrams a day for this 73-year-old woman, and then tapered over about seventeen months. By the end of the taper her proteinuria had dropped below zero point three grams a day, the blood in her urine had gone, and her creatinine stayed steady.

(Exact values: oral prednisolone 30 mg/d started 1 year after kidney biopsy; prednisolone tapered over 17 months (Case Report section); the Abstract instead describes 'two years of tapering corticosteroid therapy'; final status: urinary protein <0.3 g/d, hematuria resolved, serum creatinine stable; Table 1, present case: follow-up 48 months, outcome creatinine 1.5 mg/dL, outcome urinary protein <0.15 g/d.)

So how far can one patient take us? Let the authors answer that themselves.

The authors are refreshingly honest about how far one patient can take us. There was no repeat biopsy, so nothing confirms under the microscope what changed. Steroids came later, which muddies any attempt to credit the improvement to the JAK inhibitor by itself. And no cytokine or molecular markers were measured, so the mechanisms they discuss stay hypotheses. They say plainly that a JAK inhibitor is not a stand-in for corticosteroids, which remain the standard treatment for high-risk IgA nephropathy, and that this is one patient, not evidence of a treatment effect.

(Exact values: limitations reported by the authors: no serial kidney biopsy, 0 immunologic or molecular biomarkers available, and confounding by corticosteroids introduced 1 year after biopsy.)

Second, the useful part of a case report is the reasoning, not the ending. What travels is the order in which the questions were asked.

And third, none of these four reports is evidence that a treatment works. Each one is a careful description of what happened once.
