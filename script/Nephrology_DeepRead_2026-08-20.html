Before we get into today's episode, let's ease in with a little Small Talk connected to today's topic.

Think back to what an ordinary winter used to sound like in a children's clinic. Runny noses in the waiting room, and nobody sitting down until lunchtime.

Then, for a couple of years, that sound changed. Masks, distance, closed classrooms.

And here's the question I keep coming back to. Did those quieter years leave a trace on the illnesses we look after?

Infections of the airways have long been linked with childhood nephrotic syndrome, and earlier work suggested that something like half of relapses follow a common cold. What nobody knew was what the disease would do when a pandemic suddenly changed how much infection children were exposed to. That is the gap this Japanese group set out to look at.

An everyday infection on one side, a childhood kidney condition on the other. That's an odd pair to hold in your head at once.

That's where today's paper walks in. One open-access study, and I have the verified full text in front of me, not just the abstract.

It's in Kidney Medicine, published online in June this year, and the lead author works at Showa Medical University in Tokyo.

So why give routinely collected records a full twenty minutes? Because this is about as close to a natural experiment as our field gets, and everything turns on whether it survives a careful look.

Now, let's get started.

Let me give you the shape of the paper first, and then we'll walk through how it was built.

The authors are careful about how far they push this. What they claim is a population-level association in Japanese children: when common respiratory infections dropped sharply, new cases of nephrotic syndrome dropped too. They suggest that non-pharmaceutical measures may have contributed to the lower incidence by suppressing everyday respiratory infection, and in the same breath they say the longer comparison windows leave real uncertainty about how big the change was. They also note that the mechanism linking an ordinary airway infection to a first episode has not been fully worked out.

Notice how carefully that is worded. Two things moved in the same direction over the same stretch of time.

That is a real observation, and also the weakest kind of evidence for a cause. Everything else that changed in those years is a rival explanation.

The question underneath has been argued about for a long time, and it is genuinely hard to test. You cannot randomise a child to catch fewer colds.

Which is why a period that changed everybody's exposure at once, everywhere, is worth this much attention.

Now to the methods, because what you're allowed to conclude here is decided there.

The design is a retrospective cohort study built from a Japanese electronic health record network that covers eleven hospitals, and it includes children from six months up to fifteen years of age. The team lined up the year before the pandemic against the first pandemic year, and they deliberately ran each period from April through March so that a complete seasonal cycle sat inside both of them. That choice matters, because respiratory infection and nephrotic syndrome both have a seasonal rhythm.

(Exact values: Data source: Pediatric Medical Information Collection System (P-MICS), 11 participating hospitals and clinics in Japan, covering more than 1,130,000 children. Eligible age range: 6 months to 15 years. Prepandemic period: April 2019 - March 2020. First pandemic year: April 2020 - March 2021. Japan's first state of emergency: declared April 7, 2020 for 7 prefectures, extended nationwide April 16, 2020.)

These records weren't made for a study. The notes were written to look after children, and the question arrived years later.

That makes the work fast and cheap, and it means you can only ask what somebody happened to type at the time.

The April-to-March choice isn't decoration either. Compare a July with a January and you would be measuring the season, not the period.

The case definition is worth pausing on. The database did not hold the body-size adjusted urine measurements that guidelines ask for, so the team wrote a practical definition instead and applied it the same way at every hospital and in both periods. A new case counted only if the child had no earlier record of nephrotic syndrome, and then picked up the diagnosis code alongside a low serum albumin and heavy proteinuria. Remission needed repeated clean urine tests, and a relapse used the same laboratory thresholds as a first episode.

(Exact values: New-onset case definition: no nephrotic syndrome diagnosis before March 31, 2019, plus ICD-10 code N04 during observation, plus serum albumin <=3.0 g/dL, plus proteinuria >=2.0 g/g creatinine or qualitative >=3+ on a single measurement. Remission definition: proteinuria <0.2 g/g creatinine or negative qualitative test on >=3 occasions. Relapse definition: serum albumin <=3.0 g/dL plus proteinuria >=2.0 g/g creatinine or qualitative >=3+.)

Why spend so long on a case definition? Because in a study built from records, the definition is the measuring instrument.

If it drifts between hospitals, or between years, then what you find may be a change in labelling rather than a change in the children.

One rule, applied the same way everywhere and in both periods, is what protects a comparison like this.

For the statistics, the team counted events and fitted a negative binomial mixed model, which lets each hospital have its own baseline rate while the pandemic period is entered as the effect of interest. The negative binomial choice handles counts that are more scattered than a simple Poisson would allow. They then rebuilt the same analysis with three other count models to see whether the answer depended on that choice, and for relapses they added an offset for person-time so differences in how long children were followed would not distort the rates.

(Exact values: Primary model: negative binomial GLMM, Events ~ Period + (1|Hospital). Relapse model adds offset(log(person-years)); person-time excludes days spent in relapse. Effect measure: incidence rate ratio with 95% CI from exponentiated coefficients. Three prespecified distributional sensitivity analyses: zero-inflated Poisson, Poisson mixed model, quasi-Poisson. Significance threshold P < 0.05; R for Windows version 4.4.2.)

Counts of rare events behave badly. You see none for months, and then two turn up in the same fortnight.

Feed that pattern to a model that expects tidy, evenly spaced counts, and it will hand you back more confidence than the data have earned.

Refitting the same question under different assumptions is one way of asking whether an answer belongs to the data or to the model.

So, methods in a sentence. Routine records, one fixed rule for who counts, and a model chosen to respect how rarely these events actually arrive.

Now the results, and I want to start with the exposure rather than the disease.

A study like this only means anything if the thing you believe changed really did change.

The background picture across these eleven Japanese hospitals is striking. Respiratory tract infection diagnoses fell by roughly half in the pandemic year, and that halving held up in every sensitivity model with very tight intervals, because the counts here run into the tens of thousands. Acetaminophen prescriptions dropped by around a third over the same window. Coronavirus diagnoses went the other way, from a handful recorded at the very end of the prepandemic year to almost fourteen thousand. So there was a great deal more coronavirus, and a great deal less of everything else that makes children feverish.

(Exact values: Respiratory tract infections: 149,534 prepandemic vs 75,876 during the pandemic; mean 12,461 vs 6,323 per month; ratio 0.51; zero-inflated Poisson, Poisson mixed and quasi-Poisson sensitivity analyses each IRR 0.51 (P < 0.001). Acetaminophen prescriptions: 142,331 vs 93,200; primary IRR 0.69 (95% CI, 0.65-0.74; P < 0.001), while all three sensitivity models gave IRR 0.65 (P < 0.001). COVID-19 diagnoses: 30 near the end of the prepandemic period vs 13,893 during the pandemic year; primary IRR 396.9 (95% CI, 253.2-622.1; P < 0.001).)

Hold that as the backdrop. Everything else in the paper is measured against it.

Across the two years this eleven-hospital Japanese network recorded two hundred eighteen new cases of nephrotic syndrome in children, one hundred twenty-seven in the year before the pandemic and ninety-one in the first pandemic year, with a median age of five years in both periods. In the main model the pandemic year carried roughly a twenty-eight percent lower incidence, and the confidence interval sat entirely below one, so that difference did reach statistical significance. It is an association measured in an observational cohort, not proof that the pandemic itself lowered the incidence.

(Exact values: Total new-onset cases across both periods: 218. Prepandemic (April 2019 - March 2020): 127 cases; median age 5 years (IQR 2-10). First pandemic year (April 2020 - March 2021): 91 cases; median age 5 years (IQR 3-8). Primary negative binomial GLMM: IRR 0.72 (95% CI, 0.55-0.94; P < 0.05).)

A rate ratio below one simply means fewer events in the second period, for the same amount of time watched.

How much fewer, and how firmly you can say so, are two separate questions. They can come back with different answers.

Now the part that decides how much weight to put on that finding. Swapping the count distribution barely moved anything, and every alternative model in this Japanese cohort landed on the same effect size, so the result is not an artefact of one modelling choice. But when the researchers stretched the comparison windows out to eighteen months, the estimate drifted closer to one and the confidence interval crossed it, so it no longer reached statistical significance. The direction of the finding held. The certainty did not.

(Exact values: Zero-inflated Poisson IRR 0.72 (95% CI, 0.54-0.94; P < 0.05); Poisson mixed IRR 0.72 (95% CI, 0.55-0.94; P < 0.05); quasi-Poisson IRR 0.72 (95% CI, 0.56-0.92; P < 0.01). Extended prepandemic window, October 2018 - March 2020 vs April 2020 - March 2021: IRR 0.795 (95% CI, 0.622-1.017; P = 0.07). Two equal 18-month windows, October 2018 - March 2020 vs April 2020 - September 2021: IRR 0.836 (95% CI, 0.675-1.036; P = 0.10).)

Where an effect points and how sure you are about it are separate properties. A study can be steady about the direction and still be unable to promise that the effect is there at all.

Then the second question, which isn't about first episodes at all.

The relapse question used a different group: three hundred sixty-six Japanese children who were already in remission when the observation window opened, median age seven years. Before the pandemic they had thirty-four relapses, which works out to roughly nine relapses per hundred person-years, and during the pandemic twenty relapses, roughly six per hundred person-years. Every model pointed in the same downward direction, and every one of them had a confidence interval that included one, including when the children were split by age above and below six years. So the honest reading is that this study could not settle whether relapse risk really changed. That is not the same as showing the two periods were equal, and the authors put it down to too few relapse events rather than to a real absence of effect.

(Exact values: Relapse cohort derivation: 499 children diagnosed April 1, 2016 - March 31, 2019; 27 aged >15 years at the March 31, 2019 cutoff excluded; of the remaining 472, 106 (22.5%) not in remission and 366 (77.5%) in remission and analyzed; median age 7 years (IQR 5-10). Prepandemic: 34 relapses over 360.4 person-years = 0.094 per person-year. Pandemic: 20 relapses over 355.2 person-years = 0.056 per person-year. Primary negative binomial GLMM: IRR 0.57 (95% CI, 0.30-1.10; P = 0.09), not statistically significant. Patient- and hospital-level random intercepts: IRR 0.63 (95% CI, 0.35-1.10; P = 0.11). Patient fixed-effects Poisson: IRR 0.69 (95% CI, 0.35-1.35; P = 0.28). Age <6 years: IRR 0.605 (95% CI, 0.207-1.772); age >=6 years: IRR 0.556 (95% CI, 0.246-1.253).)

That result is easy to file away as a negative one. It isn't.

When a study cannot reach significance, the honest reading is that it was too small to tell an effect apart from ordinary variation.

Now to what this can and cannot support, starting with the limits the authors put on the record themselves.

The first limitation is the design itself. This is an observational study, so it can show that two things moved together in time; it cannot show that one produced the other. The authors underline that treating an ordinary respiratory infection as a trigger for a first episode of nephrotic syndrome is not an established paradigm but still a hypothesis. And they point out that the pandemic coronavirus might itself act as an immune trigger, which cuts against the simple story.

An association plus a plausible story is still an association. In that sentence the story is doing more work than the evidence is.

The second group of limitations is about what the database could and could not see. A child counted only if they were seen inside this hospital network, so care given elsewhere is invisible, and the team did not collect information on whether admission thresholds or diagnostic routines shifted during the pandemic. The record system also did not capture when urine was collected, so complete and partial remission could not be told apart by guideline definitions and remission status may be misclassified. And because these are Japanese children, in a country with its own social context and its own adherence to pandemic measures, the authors warn against carrying the numbers to other health systems.

Records only hold what somebody typed, in a place that belonged to the network.

And think about behaviour in those years. If families waited longer before coming in, or a department changed who it admitted, a count can fall while nothing about the illness has changed.

The last limitation is the one that most shapes how confident we should be. The result for new cases depends on where the comparison windows were drawn. The team chose those windows in advance for good reasons, to match Japan's first state of emergency and to hold complete seasonal cycles, but once they stretched the baseline and follow-up out, statistical significance faded. They read that as limited statistical power and year-to-year variability rather than a fatal flaw, and they ask for larger studies that model time trends directly.

Let me add an appraisal of my own, about what a before-and-after design can carry.

Two adjacent years give you a pair of points. Anything that has been drifting slowly across a decade will look like a step change if you only sample twice.

That is close to what the authors ask for at the end of the paper.

So where does this leave a nephrologist on Monday morning?

Nowhere different, in terms of what you do in clinic. A single before-and-after in routine records is not the kind of evidence that moves practice, and the authors don't ask it to.

What it does is give an old clinical impression something firmer to argue with, which is a real contribution.

And a wider point about natural experiments: they're opportunistic by nature, which is both their charm and their weakness.

There is no randomised version of this question to run, so an imperfect look is the only look we get. It deserves careful reading rather than casual quoting.

Before today's words and phrases, let's bring the main discussion together.

First take-home. When a finding survives a change of model but not a change of window, the fragile part is the comparison, not the arithmetic.

Direction can be stable while certainty is not, and it is certainty that decides how loudly you are allowed to speak.

Second take-home. A result that misses significance is a question left open, not an answer of no.

Saying we could not tell is less satisfying than saying nothing changed. It is also the sentence the data actually support.
