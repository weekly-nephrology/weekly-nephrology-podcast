Now, let's get started.

First, a question that tends to reach the clinic well before the evidence does. In IgA nephropathy, does adding a second kidney-protective drug on top of the first one actually buy you anything?

This report brings together two studies in IgA nephropathy. In SPARTACUS, an open-label phase two study, people already taking an SGLT2 inhibitor had their renin-angiotensin blocker swapped for sparsentan. In a substudy of the PROTECT open-label extension, people already on sparsentan were randomly assigned either to add an SGLT2 inhibitor or to stay on sparsentan alone for twelve weeks, and after that everyone could take the combination for another twenty-four weeks.

Two different angles on the same practical question, published together in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation.

SPARTACUS enrolled forty-eight adults with IgA nephropathy, average age about forty-nine, and thirty-nine of them finished treatment. After the switch to sparsentan, albuminuria dropped quickly, and the reduction was still there at week twenty-four, with a least-squares mean fall of roughly fifty-six percent. The confidence interval around that estimate is wide, so the size of the effect is less certain than the direction.

(Exact values: 48 patients enrolled; mean (SD) age 48.9 (13.9) years; 39 completed treatment; UACR least-squares mean -56% (95% CI -66% to -3%) through week 24.)

Now the part that can actually compare one option against another.

The PROTECT extension substudy included sixty-three people who completed the twelve-week randomised period: thirty-two took sparsentan together with an SGLT2 inhibitor, and thirty-one continued on sparsentan alone. Fifty-four of them went on to complete twenty-four weeks of the combination.

(Exact values: sparsentan + SGLT2i n=32; sparsentan alone n=31; 54 completed 24 weeks of sparsentan + SGLT2i.)

At twelve weeks, proteinuria was modestly lower when an SGLT2 inhibitor was added to sparsentan than when sparsentan was continued on its own. The ratio between the two groups was zero point seven five, with a confidence interval of zero point five eight to zero point nine eight, so it just excluded one, and the p value was below zero point zero five. The authors describe this as a modest further reduction rather than a large one.

(Exact values: week 12 UPCR least-squares mean -11.1% (95% CI -26.3% to 7.3%) with sparsentan + SGLT2i; 17.7% (95% CI -2.4% to 42.1%) with sparsentan alone; between-group ratio 0.75 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.98); P<0.05.)

Modest is the word the authors picked, and it is the one to hold on to.

Now the limits, and then we move on.

Two things are worth keeping in mind. The randomised comparison ran for only twelve weeks, and the SPARTACUS part was open-label with no control group. The endpoints reported here are proteinuria and safety, so this tells us nothing yet about long-term kidney survival.

Next, a complete change of scale, in the Journal of the American Society of Nephrology. One registry, many diagnoses, and an outcome that has nothing to do with urine.

This one is a large registry cohort from the China Renal Data System. The team assembled sixty-six thousand two hundred forty-seven people with glomerulonephritis, covering fourteen different underlying causes recorded between the year two thousand and twenty twenty-two, and then compared deaths from cardiovascular disease, from ischaemic heart disease and from stroke across those causes.

Start with the plain count, because it sets the floor for everything after it.

Over follow-up there were nine hundred seventy-one cardiovascular deaths, which is about one percent of the whole cohort. Within that, three hundred thirty-seven deaths were attributed to ischaemic heart disease and two hundred eighty-six to stroke.

(Exact values: 971 cardiovascular deaths (1%); 337 ischemic heart disease-associated deaths (0.5%); 286 stroke-associated deaths (0.4%).)

Now the same cohort, measured against the general population.

Crude cardiovascular death rates varied enormously between subtypes, from well under one to more than twenty per thousand person-years. Compared with the general population, every glomerular disease subtype carried a higher risk of cardiovascular death. Lupus nephritis sat at the top, with a standardised mortality ratio close to twenty-eight, and minimal change disease at the bottom, close to four.

(Exact values: crude cardiovascular death rates 0.7 to 22.6 per 1000 person-years; lupus nephritis indirect standardized mortality ratio 27.9; minimal change disease indirect standardized mortality ratio 3.91.)

And the reading the authors settle on.

Taken together, the authors describe cardiovascular risk that is raised right across glomerular disease, but that differs a great deal depending on the underlying cause. Because this is an observational cohort, what we have is an association between the type of glomerular disease and cardiovascular death, not proof that one drives the other.

The design sets the limits here. This is retrospective registry data, and the abstract gives us crude rates and standardised ratios without telling us how individual risk factors or treatments were accounted for, so the differences between subtypes are best read as descriptive.

What to do with raised risk is exactly where the next paper starts, in the same journal. This one is guidance, not new data.

The American Society of Nephrology has issued guidance to sit alongside the first joint cardiology, diabetes and nephrology guideline on what is now called cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome. Its central ask is that we assess cardiovascular risk, metabolic health and kidney function together rather than in separate lanes, with kidney disease treated as central rather than peripheral.

So that is the framing. Then come the specifics.

For risk assessment, the guidance asks us to use cardiovascular risk equations in people with CKD, naming the PREVENT equation specifically, and to pair that with kidney outcome predictors when we counsel patients and decide on preventive therapy.

On treatment, the foundation stays familiar: renin-angiotensin system blockade plus an SGLT2 inhibitor to protect heart and kidney together. On top of that base, GLP-1-based drugs and a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist can be layered in, chosen according to the individual patient's cardiovascular and metabolic risk.

And then, to its credit, the document turns on itself.

There is an honest caveat inside the document itself. The evidence behind these cardiokidney therapies thins out in glomerulonephritis, in transplant recipients, in people on maintenance dialysis, in children, in older adults and in frailty. The guidance still supports screening and diagnosis in those groups, with treatment decided together with the patient.

That paragraph is the one I would read twice.

Now back to the clinic conversation I opened with, because there is a paper sitting behind it, in the Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology.

Current guidance, as summarised here, points in a different direction: plant-forward patterns such as a Mediterranean style diet, more fruit and vegetables, and attention to potassium and phosphorus additives in ultraprocessed food. The argument is that better overall diet quality helps slow CKD and also helps with diabetes, blood pressure, weight, lipids, oedema and electrolytes.

Medical nutrition therapy here means something more structured than handing over a diet sheet. A registered dietitian assesses the medical history, the laboratory results and the medicines, and also food access and food preferences, and then delivers the counselling and education that actually shifts adherence.

That is a bigger job than the word diet usually suggests.

Which raises the obvious question. If that service already exists, is anyone using it?

The practical gap they highlight is uptake. In the United States most insurance, including Medicare, covers this service, and yet it stays underused. Their argument is that using it more would lift the quality of CKD care, though that expectation is a projection rather than a measured result.

It is worth being clear about what this article is. It is a perspective piece rather than a trial or a cohort, so it builds a case from guidelines and practice experience rather than from new outcome data, and the points about insurance coverage describe the American system.

A short paper, and a clear position.

Next, lupus nephritis, and a paper in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation that is openly trying to change somebody's mind.

The authors open with an uncomfortable fact. Lupus nephritis is still one of the most severe faces of lupus, and although treatment options have expanded a great deal over the past twenty years, the number of patients who reach end-stage kidney disease or who die has not meaningfully shifted.

Their explanation is relapse. Every flare leaves more damage behind and pushes the patient further along toward chronic kidney disease, and that is why protocol biopsies have become more common: to judge who is likely to relapse, and to decide when immunosuppression can be stopped.

Here is where they part company with the current direction of travel.

Their central argument is about where to look. A protocol biopsy assumes the answer sits in the kidney, but they see the kidney as showing the downstream consequences of what the immune system is doing upstream.

And then the sentence that gives the paper its edge.

After going through the published work on repeat protocol biopsy, their conclusion is blunt. They believe the evidence behind the practice is faulty and that the benefit in class three or four disease is limited. That is their reading of the literature, and it runs against a practice that has been growing.

What they propose instead is serological monitoring: anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies for immunological activity, alongside C3 and C4 complement levels for clinical activity, used together to estimate relapse risk and to time any reduction or withdrawal of immunosuppression. This is the authors' proposal, not a guideline position.

It is important to label what this piece is. A narrative review putting forward the authors' own paradigm shift, without new patient data and without a direct comparison of biopsy-guided against serology-guided management.

A position, argued in public, and labelled as one. That last part matters.

Now something much smaller and much more specific, in Clinical and Experimental Nephrology, and it comes from a single hospital.

This is a small retrospective series from a single centre. The team gathered twelve patients who developed nephrotic syndrome after a liver transplant and who went on to have a kidney biopsy, then looked at what the biopsies showed, how they were treated and how they did afterwards.

Now the part people usually want to know.

The histology was mixed. IgA nephropathy was the most common, in five of the twelve. Then two had membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, two had diabetic nephropathy, and there was one each with thrombotic microangiopathy, pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis and membranous nephropathy.

And then the follow-up.

Over roughly six years of follow-up, eight of the twelve reached what the authors call incomplete remission, meaning proteinuria under three and a half grams per gram. Three patients progressed to end-stage kidney disease: one with IgA nephropathy, one with membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis and one with crescentic glomerulonephritis.

(Exact values: follow-up period 5.8 years; 8 patients (67%) achieved incomplete remission (<3.5 g/gCr); 3 patients progressed to end-stage kidney disease.)

The obvious limits are size and selection. Twelve patients from one centre, all of them included precisely because they had a biopsy, so the mix of diagnoses here should not be read as the true distribution of nephrotic syndrome after liver transplantation.

Which is about as far as a series this size can take anyone.

And finally, vascular access. Today's last paper is in The Lancet Digital Health, and it is a proper randomised trial.

This was a phase three randomised trial run across thirty-one American centres. Patients dialysing through a catheter, who were candidates for either a fistula or a graft, were randomly assigned in the operating room, before the first incision, either to a standard autogenous fistula or to a bioengineered acellular vessel. Nobody was masked. The coprimary outcomes were functional patency at six months and secondary patency at twelve months, brought together into a single measure the investigators call global relative patency.

Between two thousand seventeen and two thousand twenty-three, two hundred forty-two patients were enrolled. Roughly three in ten were women. One hundred nineteen were assigned to a fistula and one hundred twenty-three to the engineered vessel.

(Exact values: 242 patients enrolled; 70 (29%) female; 119 (49%) assigned to autogenous AVF; 123 (51%) assigned to ATEV; enrolment from Sept 29, 2017 to April 22, 2023.)

And the headline result.

The trial met its coprimary endpoints in favour of the engineered vessel. The global relative patency was one point one seven, with a confidence interval from one point zero four to one point three one, and a p value of zero point zero zero nine.

(Exact values: global relative patency 1.17 (95% CI 1.04-1.31); p=0.0090.)

Infection rates were similar in the two groups, but the engineered vessel needed more interventions overall to keep it working. The investigators also describe a larger difference in one subgroup, made up of women and of men who had both diabetes and obesity, where the patency measure was one point six six. Being a subgroup, that is best treated as hypothesis-generating.

(Exact values: subgroup global relative patency 1.66 (95% CI 1.34-2.04).)

A few caveats before we move on. The trial was open-label, so the surgeon, the patient and the site staff all knew which access had been used, and the funding came from the company behind the vessel. The abstract also does not tell us whether that subgroup analysis was planned in advance.
