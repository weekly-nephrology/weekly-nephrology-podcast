The authors explain that changes in the inverted formin-2 gene, usually shortened to INF2, are a recognised cause of inherited focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and also of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, and that changes in exon 6 of this gene have only rarely been reported.

Hold that in a corner of your mind.

In this single case, a boy first developed protein in the urine when he was twelve, and a kidney biopsy two years later gave him a diagnosis of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis.

(Exact values: proteinuria from age 12 years; focal segmental glomerulosclerosis on kidney biopsy at age 14 years.)

So far, nothing unusual.

Steroids, and then further immunosuppressive treatment including plasma exchange, did not work in this boy.

One treatment that fails can be bad luck. A whole sequence of them is information.

When he was fifteen, genetic testing identified a heterozygous missense change in exon 6 of the INF2 gene.

(Exact values: at age 15 years, a heterozygous missense variant of INF2 exon 6, c.763G>A (p.Asp255Asn).)

That's the turning point, and it's worth marking.

From here you have to work backwards.

Even with conservative management, he reached end-stage kidney disease at seventeen, and the authors describe this as a relatively aggressive kidney course.

(Exact values: end-stage kidney disease at age 17 years, roughly five years on from the first sign of proteinuria.)

And here's what this abstract leaves open.

The abstract never tells us why the steroids, the immunosuppressive drugs that followed, and the plasma exchange all failed to work in this boy. No mechanism is offered for that lack of response. And variants in exon 6 of INF2 have been described so rarely that his trajectory cannot show how variants in that part of the gene behave more generally.

Next, a case where the timing is the whole point.

Also in CEN Case Reports, from the same early September batch.

The patient here was a man in his late sixties with advanced lung cancer that could not be removed surgically, and during durvalumab treatment he developed organizing pneumonia in the lungs and then nephrotic syndrome. The striking part of the timing is that the heavy proteinuria appeared while he was already taking glucocorticoids for the lung problem.

(Exact values: man, age 68 years; durvalumab for advanced non-small cell lung cancer that was unresectable.)

Read the order of events again.

Now to what the biopsy showed.

The kidney biopsy in this patient was not a single clean pattern. It showed lesions that looked like focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, together with marked effacement and detachment of the podocyte foot processes and prominent inflammation in the tubules and interstitium, and on that basis the authors diagnosed durvalumab-associated nephrotic syndrome.

So what happens next?

His proteinuria then improved after durvalumab was stopped and he was given losartan, dapagliflozin and finerenone, and the authors point out that this happened without a substantial increase in his glucocorticoid dose.

So what can the authors actually say here?

The authors read this course as a reminder of how varied durvalumab-associated nephrotic syndrome can look, both in its clinical timing and in what the biopsy shows.

Varied is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

And two gaps sit underneath it.

The abstract never says how long this man was followed, so we have no idea over what stretch of time the proteinuria improved. And the durvalumab was stopped in the same period that three new drugs, losartan, dapagliflozin and finerenone, were all started, so the improvement cannot be credited to the withdrawal of the checkpoint inhibitor or to the new treatments; those changes are tangled together in the report.

Now to the case that travels furthest from our own clinic.

Also CEN Case Reports, this one published at the end of June.

The authors describe Purtscher-like retinopathy as a rare blockage-type disease of the small retinal vessels that causes sudden, painless loss of vision with a characteristic appearance at the back of the eye. It is usually linked to trauma, but it has also been reported in systemic conditions such as nephrotic syndrome, where a tendency to clot, complement activation and tiny emboli have been proposed as the drivers.

That's the background. Now to the child.

In this single case a three-year-old girl who already had steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome suddenly lost her vision. She could no longer fix her gaze and her eyes drifted outwards at times, and there had been no trauma and her blood pressure was not high. Looking at the back of the eye, there was whitening of the retina around both optic discs with sparing next to the small arterioles, along with cotton wool spots and bleeding within the retina.

(Exact values: age 3 years; the fundoscopic findings at presentation were bilateral.)

This is where a nephrologist borrows somebody else's expertise, fast.

Now the laboratory work and the medicines, which narrow down what's left.

Her blood tests showed a low albumin of about one point seven grams per decilitre together with nephrotic-range protein loss, and the kidney biopsy pointed to a podocytopathy. She had been taking prednisolone with cyclosporine, and the cyclosporine was stopped because the team was concerned about drug-induced injury to the small vessels.

(Exact values: serum albumin 1.7 g/dL, with proteinuria in the nephrotic range; cyclosporine trough 84 ng/mL when she presented.)

A decision made under real uncertainty.

Then the workup goes a layer deeper.

She was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone pulses and then an oral taper, and whole-exome sequencing found a pathogenic variant in the WT1 gene that had arisen in her rather than being inherited from either parent, which gave a genetic explanation for her steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome.

(Exact values: pathogenic WT1 variant c.1447 + 5G > A, de novo, meaning it arose in the child and came from neither parent; reported as establishing a genetic aetiology (NPHS4).)

Which quietly changes the frame around the whole admission.

And the outcome, as reported.

By six months her vision had improved from only being able to perceive light to counting fingers at about two metres, so the recovery was real but partial.

(Exact values: visual acuity at presentation, light perception only; at 6 months, counting fingers at 2 m.)

The authors' take-home is that sudden visual loss in a child with nephrotic syndrome can come from Purtscher-like retinopathy, especially when the nephrotic syndrome is steroid-resistant or a calcineurin inhibitor is being used, and that in their patient a prompt eye assessment and a careful look at the medicines were central to what happened next.

And here's what this report can't pull apart.

Because this is one small child, described from the abstract of a case report with a literature review, we cannot say how often this eye complication happens, and we cannot untangle how much the nephrotic syndrome itself, the underlying genetic disease and the cyclosporine each contributed, since the drug was stopped just as the steroid pulses were started.

And now the case I've read in full.

This one's in Kidney Medicine, in the July issue.

Full text means we can follow the timeline and the reasoning, not just the summary.

The authors describe IgA-dominant infection-related glomerulonephritis as a rare form of acute post-infectious glomerulonephritis that mostly affects older adults who already have other chronic illnesses, and that is usually reported after skin infections or abscesses inside the body, with acute kidney injury, protein and blood in the urine, and low complement levels.

Now the patient, and the first admission.

The patient in this report was a man in his late sixties who was taking methotrexate and adalimumab for rheumatoid arthritis and also had sarcoidosis. He came to hospital after about two weeks of fever, night sweats, weight loss and nausea, imaging showed a large abscess in his spleen, and a culture taken from that abscess grew Mycobacterium avium, so his immunosuppressive drugs were stopped and antimycobacterial treatment was started.

(Exact values: man, age 68 years, with stage II sarcoidosis and rheumatoid arthritis on methotrexate and adalimumab; nausea, fever and night sweats over 2 weeks, plus 6 kg weight loss; first admission, splenic abscess 37 x 36 x 37 mm on ultrasound.)

So far this is an infection story, start to finish.

Then it changes.

About two weeks later this same patient was readmitted because the splenic abscess had grown despite antimycobacterial treatment, and by then his kidneys were involved as well. His creatinine had risen from a normal starting value, his urine was full of dysmorphic red cells with red cell casts, and he was losing close to six grams of protein a day, while his complement levels stayed normal and tests for antinuclear, ANCA and anti-glomerular basement membrane antibodies were all negative.

(Exact values: serum creatinine 0.80 mg/dL at first admission, 1.32 mg/dL at readmission; urinalysis, red blood cells >400 per field of view, of which >50% dysmorphic, plus acanthocytes and red blood cell casts; proteinuria 5.8 g/24 h; C3 956 mg/L, C4 293 mg/L, both reported as normal; splenic abscess at readmission, 56 x 49 x 42 mm.)

A lot in one breath.

This is where the kidney becomes the main question.

When the creatinine reached one point nine milligrams per decilitre, the team suspected an infection-related glomerulonephritis on clinical grounds and started oral prednisolone at one milligram per kilogram, which came to eighty milligrams a day. The kidney biopsy came after that, and its purpose was to establish what the underlying diagnosis actually was. A week into the steroids kidney function had improved only slightly. The abscess was drained through the stomach and the steroid dose was tapered, but fever and inflammatory markers persisted and the creatinine rose to around three milligrams per decilitre, so the spleen was removed. Eleven days after that operation the creatinine had come down to about one point eight and he went home.

(Exact values: oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg (80 mg) begun at serum creatinine 1.90 mg/dL, on suspicion of infection-related glomerulonephritis, ahead of the kidney biopsy; before splenectomy, peak serum creatinine 3.09 mg/dL; 11 days after splenectomy, serum creatinine 1.82 mg/dL.)

Notice how many things were moving at once.

And notice where the biopsy sits in it.

The kidney biopsy in this patient came after prednisolone had already been started, and it was done to establish what was actually driving the kidney disease. It contained twenty-two glomeruli, none of them globally scarred, and it showed diffuse mild endocapillary proliferation with some double contours. The immunofluorescence was dominated by IgA and C3 with only traces of the other immunoglobulins and polyclonal light chains, and electron microscopy showed deposits in the mesangium and under the endothelium with widespread foot process effacement, but no subepithelial humps. Taken together, the authors read this as IgA-dominant infection-related glomerulonephritis.

(Exact values: 22 glomeruli sampled, with none globally sclerosed; on immunofluorescence, IgA 2+ and C3 2+, with trace IgG, IgM and C1q, and kappa 1+ and lambda 1+ (polyclonal).)

Dense reporting, and their reading sits in the last sentence.

It would be tempting to tell this as a clean story. The record doesn't let us.

Then the follow-up, which doesn't run in a straight line.

What followed was not a straight line. Eleven days after the splenectomy his creatinine was one point eight two milligrams per decilitre and he was discharged, but two weeks later he was back in hospital with rising inflammation and kidney function that was slipping again. The scan at that point showed more fluid in the abdomen, the abscesses already known in the splenic bed had grown, and fresh ones had appeared, which the authors took as the Mycobacterium avium infection advancing. His oral steroid dose was increased, and he left hospital before anything further could be worked up. Two months after that he was admitted again, this time with fever and an abscess in the old splenic bed that grew gut bacteria, with no Mycobacterium avium detectable any more, and through that admission his kidney function held, with creatinine down to one point two eight. By the end of the antimycobacterial course, five months further on, it was one point one three milligrams per decilitre.

(Exact values: at discharge, 11 days after splenectomy, serum creatinine 1.82 mg/dL; readmitted 2 weeks after that discharge with rising inflammation, worsening kidney function and CT progression of the M. avium infection, and oral glucocorticoids increased; serum creatinine 1.28 mg/dL at the admission 2 months later for the intestinal-flora abscess, then 1.13 mg/dL at the end of antimycobacterial therapy 5 months after that.)

Worth holding on to that whole sequence.

Here's their reading of the whole thing.

The authors present this as the first reported link between Mycobacterium avium infection and IgA-dominant infection-related glomerulonephritis, and they suggest that the large amount of mycobacterial antigen still sitting in the splenic abscess kept driving the immune complex injury, which is why removing the source mattered in their patient. They also point out that guidelines do not generally recommend immunosuppression for infection-related glomerulonephritis, and that steroids were used here because kidney function was falling while source control was proving difficult.

(Exact values: the authors cite KDIGO 2021 guidance, under which immunosuppression is not recommended for infection-related glomerulonephritis; their Table 1 collects 3 previously published cases of M. avium infection with infection-related glomerulonephritis, and none of those showed IgA-dominant staining.)

Notice how carefully that's phrased. Plausible is the word they earn, and they stop there.

Three things, and then the limit under them.

First, the order in which things happen is evidence in its own right.

Second, when one thing is stopped and others are started in the same week, the credit is shared.

Third, the finding that reframes a kidney case often arrives from a clinic that isn't ours.

And now the limit.

Since this is one patient who received antimycobacterial drugs, steroids, drainage and then splenectomy one after another, the report can support a hypothesis about infection-driven glomerulonephritis, but it cannot prove cause and effect or tell us how much each step contributed, and the authors themselves say more work is needed.
