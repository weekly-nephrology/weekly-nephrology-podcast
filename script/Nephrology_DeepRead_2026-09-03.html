If a patient with kidney disease has just been started on acid suppression, does coming off it early, soon after starting, change where their kidneys end up?

And notice the shape of that question. It isn't only about the decision to begin a medicine. It's about the decision that comes just after, whether to keep going with it.

The clean answer would come from a randomised trial. Today's paper is not a trial, so the real question is how close you can get without one.

This was a retrospective cohort study drawn from five nephrology outpatient clinics in Osaka, Japan, and the investigators applied a target trial emulation framework to adults with chronic kidney disease who were not on dialysis and who newly started either a proton pump inhibitor or an H2 blocker; on average these patients were in their early seventies with a filtration rate around thirty, and the outcome being counted was reaching dialysis or transplantation.

A target trial emulation is a way of thinking more than it is a single technique. You write down the trial you wish somebody had run, and then you try to build that trial out of records you already have.

The discipline is the useful half. Writing the protocol first stops you from quietly picking whichever version of the analysis gives the tidiest answer.

Routine records are not neutral, by the way. They were written in order to care for a person, and not in order to answer a question, and every study built backwards out of them has to live with that.

Now to the methods, because this is where a paper like this either earns your trust or loses it.

We'll take two analyses in turn, and they answer different questions, so it's worth keeping them apart as we go.

The first analysis is the easier of them to picture. You compare people who start a medicine with people who start a different medicine for the same job, and you follow both.

That's what an active comparator means, and it's the honest version of this kind of comparison. Compare a medicine against an untreated group, and a good part of what you end up measuring is simply who gets prescribed medicines in the first place.

There's a second piece of hygiene in that design, and it hides in the words new user. A new user design keeps out anyone who was already taking the medicine when the clock started.

That matters more than it sounds. People who have tolerated a medicine for years are, in a quiet way, survivors of it, and letting them in flatters the medicine.

The second analysis is stranger, and it's the one worth slowing down for.

How do you study stopping, when the decision to stop is made by a patient and a clinician, and not by a protocol?

To compare stopping with continuing, the team copied every patient into both strategies, censored each copy at the moment its real prescription record stopped matching the strategy it had been assigned, and then re-weighted the remaining follow-up so the two arms stayed comparable; early stopping meant coming off the drug inside a ninety-day window, and this whole construction exists to keep an observational dataset from generating immortal time bias.

(Exact values: Grace period defining early discontinuation: 90 days after initiation; discontinuation defined as no prescription for a period of >= 30 days following the previous supply; 95% CIs from nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 500 iterations; missing baseline covariates handled by multiple imputation by chained equations, 5 imputed datasets combined by Rubin's rules; statistical significance defined as 2-sided P < 0.05.)

Take that apart slowly, because three separate ideas are packed into it.

A grace period is simply the window you allow for the decision to be made. Real prescribing isn't instantaneous, so the protocol has to say how long a person may take before they count as somebody who stopped.

Artificial censoring sounds violent, and it isn't. It only means that a copy stops contributing data at the point where the record and the assigned strategy part company.

And immortal time bias is the trap all of that is built to avoid. If somebody has to survive a while before they can be counted as a stopper, then stoppers begin the race with a head start that has nothing to do with the medicine.

The cloning step removes that head start, because every person begins in both arms at the very same moment.

The weighting step is the part you have to take on faith. Weighting only works if the variables somebody happened to record really do explain who left and who stayed.

So the methods takeaway is about reading rather than about kidneys. Everything that follows is an estimate produced by a model, and the model's assumptions travel with the number wherever that number goes.

Now the results, in that same order, and give each of them a moment.

I'll give you those numbers in words rather than in decimals. The exact figures sit on the page for this episode, where you can read them slowly instead of catching them in traffic.

Over a median of about two years of follow-up, the patients who began a proton pump inhibitor reached dialysis or transplantation more often than the patients who began an H2 blocker, with a weighted hazard ratio close to one point four; because nobody was randomly assigned, that number is an adjusted estimate of association under the study's weighting assumptions, not a demonstration that the drug itself drove the difference.

(Exact values: Median follow-up 2.1 years (IQR 0.7-4.6); KFRT events 509 of 3512 PPI new users and 145 of 1102 H2 blocker new users, 654 of 4614 overall (14.2%); weighted incidence rate of KFRT 49.7 (95% CI 45.4-54.4) per 1000 person-years with PPIs versus 33.8 (95% CI 28.0-41.0) with H2 blockers; weighted HR 1.39 (95% CI 1.13-1.72), unweighted HR 1.57 (95% CI 1.31-1.89).)

Hold on to the word estimate in that sentence, because it's doing real work there.

Why not simply say the medicine did it? Because the people who received it were not a random half of the room, and no amount of weighting can prove that they were.

There's an asymmetry in how we usually read the two directions, too. A raised hazard from an observational comparison gets called a warning, and a lowered one gets called a benefit. Both of them deserve exactly the same suspicion.

Now the second analysis, which is the one the title of the paper is really about.

Among the patients who had started a proton pump inhibitor, roughly three in ten came off it inside the first ninety days, and the weighted analysis put the five-year risk of kidney failure about three percentage points lower under the stopping strategy than under the continuing strategy; that gap is an estimate that only holds if the measured variables really captured why some people stopped and others did not.

(Exact values: 4187 PPI initiators eligible for the clone-censor-weighting analysis, of whom 1254 (30.0%) discontinued within 90 days and 2933 continued beyond 90 days; KFRT events in the emulated trial 176 discontinuation replicates and 388 continuation replicates; estimated 5-year absolute risk of KFRT 18.8% (95% CI 16.4-21.2) with discontinuation versus 21.9% (95% CI 20.3-23.6) with continuation; absolute risk difference -3.0% (95% CI -5.7 to -0.6); hazard ratio 0.85 (95% CI 0.72-0.97).)

Notice what kind of number that is. Not a ratio, but a difference in how many people end up in the same place, which is usually the shape a patient can actually use.

And notice the conditional clause at the end. It isn't decoration. It's the price of admission for every number in this half of the paper.

Before we let any of that settle, look at what happens when you push on it.

A sensitivity analysis is where you find out whether a finding is a property of the data or a property of the definition you happened to choose.

Every finding has dials. What counts as stopping is a dial. How long you allow for the decision is another. A result that survives being turned is stronger than a result that does not.

Death from any cause looked similar under the two strategies, but the confidence interval was wide enough to include a real difference in either direction, so this study cannot tell us that stopping changes survival; and the kidney signal itself faded once the window for stopping was widened, no longer reaching statistical significance when that window stretched to six months or more.

(Exact values: All-cause mortality, discontinuation versus continuation: estimated 5-year risk 28.0% (95% CI 25.1-30.8) versus 27.3% (95% CI 25.5-29.1); risk difference 0.7% (95% CI -1.9 to 3.4); HR 1.03 (95% CI 0.92-1.15); deaths in the emulated trial 358 discontinuation replicates and 523 continuation replicates; negative control exposure, H2 blocker discontinuation versus continuation, KFRT HR 1.02 (95% CI 0.76-1.30) and all-cause mortality HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.75-1.11); PPI versus H2 blocker initiation, all-cause mortality weighted HR 1.10 (95% CI 0.93-1.30).)

A wide interval sitting around no difference is not a finding of no difference. It's a shrug, and it should be reported as one.

The second half of that is the more sobering part. A signal that thins out as the window widens is weaker than one that holds however far you turn the dial, and honest papers show you the dial.

A limitations paragraph is the most honest part of most papers, and it is also the part that gets read the least.

Theirs first.

The authors are careful to say that this design cannot establish cause and effect, because nobody was randomly assigned, and unmeasured differences between the people who stayed on the drug and the people who came off it could still account for part of the gap; on top of that, treatment was inferred from prescription records, and the reasons for starting or stopping were never available to the investigators.

That's their list, and it's a candid list. Here are a couple of small things I'd add as a reader.

The first is about language. The word causal sits in the title of this paper, and the limitations paragraph inside the very same paper says the design cannot deliver it. If the title is all you ever see, you'll walk away remembering something the study did not show.

The second is a habit of arithmetic restraint. When a paper runs separate analyses over differently defined groups, resist the urge to line them up against each other and subtract. Those groups were built to answer different questions, and the gap between them is not a dropout rate.

None of that is a reason to dismiss the work. It's a reason to hold it at the strength it was actually offered at.

So where does this leave you, standing in front of a medication list on an ordinary clinic afternoon?

Not with a new rule. Nothing here tells you to stop anything for the patient in front of you.

The authors' take-home is that a proton pump inhibitor without a clear indication deserves the same periodic reassessment we give any other long-term drug in chronic kidney disease, and that deprescribing it can be treated as part of a kidney protection strategy, though they add that an interventional study is still needed before the effect can be called causal.

Reassessment and withdrawal are not the same word, and it's worth keeping them apart when you repeat this.

And the boundary of this evidence is narrower than the headline is going to be. Widening it is where papers like this one usually go wrong in the retelling.

What a study like this can change is smaller and more useful than a rule. It can change what you notice. It can make a freshly added line on a list feel like a question still open, rather than a settled fact.
