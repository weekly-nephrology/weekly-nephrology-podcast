Second case, and a change of setting, from the tubules and interstitium to the glomerulus. It's in CEN Case Reports, published in July, from the nephrology department at Hiroshima University Hospital in Japan.

A quick reminder about the disease first, and then the patient.

Immunotactoid glomerulopathy is a rare kidney disease in which electron microscopy shows tiny hollow tubules deposited in the glomeruli, and immunofluorescence typically shows IgG, C3 and a single light-chain type. It often travels with blood cancers such as lymphoma or lymphocytic leukemia.

Now the patient, and the workup.

A seventy-one-year-old woman had heavy proteinuria, a protein-to-creatinine ratio of about four point three grams per gram, and a creatinine of about one point two. On her biopsy, standard frozen-section immunofluorescence lit up for C3 and kappa, but IgG was negative. Electron microscopy, though, showed hollow microtubules of fifteen to twenty nanometers lined up in parallel, which pointed toward immunotactoid disease. So the two tests did not quite match.

(Exact values: Age: 71 years; UPCR 4.32 g/gCr; serum creatinine 1.24 mg/dL; Frozen IF: C3+ and kappa+ along GBM, IgG negative; EM: hollow-centered microtubules 15-20 nm diameter, parallel arrays.)

That mismatch is the pivot of the whole case. So what did the pathologists do with it?

To resolve that mismatch, the pathologists re-stained paraffin sections after protease digestion and also used mass spectrometry. Both found IgG2 along the capillary walls, so the IgG had been hidden, or masked, on the frozen sections. The final diagnosis was monoclonal immunotactoid glomerulopathy with masked IgG. Because she also had a paraprotein in blood and urine, a bone marrow exam was done and showed symptomatic multiple myeloma.

From the diagnosis to the outcome.

After myeloma chemotherapy, her M-protein came down, proteinuria fell below half a gram per gram, and her kidney function held steady. The abstract does not say which regimen was used.

(Exact values: Proteinuria after chemotherapy: <0.5 g/gCr.)

And finally, the lesson the authors themselves chose to draw.

The authors' take-home message was about pathology technique: when the immunofluorescence and electron microscopy tell different stories, and a false-negative immunoglobulin stain is suspected, they suggest re-staining paraffin tissue or turning to mass spectrometry, to make sure the diagnosis and treatment are right. In this patient, that step revealed the masked IgG2 deposits.

The abstract does not give the chemotherapy regimen, the details of her myeloma, or how long she was followed, so we can't say how durable the kidney response was.

Third case, and a metabolic story this time. It's in BMC Nephrology, published in June, from the CHUM Research Center in Montréal, Canada.

A little background first, and then the patient.

Xanthinuria is a rare inherited condition in which the enzyme xanthine dehydrogenase is missing, so xanthine piles up in the urine. It is known for causing stones and, only rarely, kidney failure, and according to the authors no one had previously described what the kidney tissue looks like.

Here's how he first presented.

A thirty-eight-year-old man came in with flank pain and a staghorn stone in his right kidney, and his creatinine was already raised at about one ninety-two micromoles per liter. The stone was removed percutaneously and turned out to be pure xanthine, and genetic testing found two disease-causing variants in the XDH gene, confirming type one xanthinuria.

(Exact values: Age: 38 years; Serum creatinine at presentation: 192 umol/L; Stone composition: 100% xanthine (infrared analysis); Genetics: 2 pathogenic XDH variants (type I xanthinuria).)

You might expect the story to end there. Here's where it turns.

Even after the stone was cleared, his kidney function kept worsening over the next months, with creatinine reaching about two thirty-eight and marked albuminuria. Imaging found no leftover stones, and the left kidney was still free of stones. A biopsy of that left kidney showed advanced scarring, but, strikingly, no xanthine crystals at all.

(Exact values: Serum creatinine after progression: 238 umol/L.)

Which leaves an obvious question hanging. What was driving the scarring?

Looking for an explanation, the team stained the tissue for COX2, an enzyme that helps make prostaglandins. Tubular COX2 was clearly lower than in biopsies from healthy people and from other patients with advanced CKD. The authors put this forward as a possible mechanism, in line with animal work, rather than a proven cause.

(Exact values: Tubular COX2 (cyclooxygenase-2) expression: considerably reduced vs healthy-control and advanced-CKD biopsies (qualitative; number of controls not reported).)

And then the longer view.

He reached kidney failure at forty-one, received a kidney transplant, and the graft was still working very well thirty-two months later.

(Exact values: Age at kidney failure: 41 years; Excellent graft function at 32 months post-transplant.)

The abstract does not say how many comparison biopsies were used or how the staining was measured, so the COX2 story is an interesting hypothesis, not an established explanation.

Now the case I've read in full. It's in Clinical Kidney Journal, published in August, from the nephrology department at Uniklinik RWTH Aachen in Germany.

I'll take it in order, from the first diagnosis to the last follow-up, and I'll keep the story and the appraisal apart.

As you listen, keep two things in view: the protein in the urine, and the kidney function itself. It helps to track them separately.

Start with the history, because the timeline matters here.

The patient was a young man whose IgA nephropathy was first found at age twenty-six, when his eGFR was already about thirty-two. He reached kidney failure roughly three years later and received a living-donor transplant. For six years his graft ran steady, with an eGFR near forty-four and no proteinuria. Let's step out of the timeline for a moment and look at how the authors explained all this.

The authors' reading was that Nefecon added to his overall steroid burden, even though it is designed to act mainly in the gut. Next, sparsentan and empagliflozin were started at the same time, with his prednisolone dose left unchanged. Over the following year his eGFR settled at around twenty-five, the albumin-to-creatinine ratio came down to about two hundred fifty, and his HbA1c returned to six percent. His breathlessness went away, he lost six kilograms, and his blood pressure improved enough to cut back on his diuretic and drop amlodipine. (Exact values: Sparsentan + empagliflozin started 2 months after Nefecon discontinuation; After ~1 year: eGFR ~25 ml/min; ACR 250 mg/g; HbA1c 6.0%; weight -6 kg; Prednisolone 5 mg/day unchanged; semaglutide discontinued; torasemide reduced; amlodipine stopped.)

That's the outcome. This is where the story stops and the appraisal starts, and with a sequence like this one, the appraisal matters more than usual.

The authors were candid about the limits. The two drugs were given one after the other, not side by side, with just a two-month gap, so a lingering Nefecon effect can't be ruled out. Empagliflozin was started together with sparsentan, so the later drop in albuminuria can't be credited to one agent. And recurrent IgA nephropathy on its own could explain the loss of graft function. So this case can't tell us which drug is better after transplant.
