Let's start somewhere ordinary. One of today's papers opens on a problem you already know, and that's a good way in.

The authors set this case up around a problem most nephrologists meet regularly. When someone has advanced kidney disease and heart disease at the same time, fluid management is hard, and it gets harder when the congestion in front of you does not seem to be fully explained either by the heart or by the kidneys.

And that's the genuinely hard part. Knowing when to push on with the plan you've got, and when the plan itself answers the wrong question.

So, today's four cases. Three I know only from the published abstract, so I'll stay inside what it says. The fourth I've read in full, and that's where most of our time goes.

Now, let's get started.

First case, from CEN Case Reports. A fluid story, and it picks up where we just left off.

The patient was an elderly man with type two diabetes, stage four chronic kidney disease, high blood pressure and ischaemic heart disease. After a transcatheter aortic valve implantation he built up fluid that would not shift: pleural effusion, ascites and swelling of the legs. When they measured pressures directly with a right heart catheter, his cardiac output was preserved, while the echocardiogram pointed to right-sided pressure overload and congestion on the venous side.

(Exact values: Stage 4 chronic kidney disease; Type 2 diabetes mellitus.)

So that's the setup. Here's where the workup stops behaving.

At first the ascites was put down to cardiorenal syndrome, or possibly liver disease. Then they tapped it, and the fluid itself was the clue. It was thick, it was loaded with protein, the serum-ascites albumin gradient was low rather than high, and the carcinoembryonic antigen level was markedly raised. An MRI without contrast then showed multiple walled-off cystic collections with fluid levels inside them. Put together, that pointed towards a mucinous process in the peritoneum, the picture of pseudomyxoma peritonei, even though the cytology came back negative.

That's the moment this case turns. Now, the edges of what we actually know.

What we are working from here is an abstract, and it leaves the key question open. The mucinous peritoneal process was suspected from the fluid and the imaging, but the cytology was negative and no tissue diagnosis is described, so the label stays provisional. The abstract also does not give us the actual numbers behind the fluid analysis, tells us nothing about what was done for the peritoneal disease, and stops before telling us how long he was followed or how he ended up.

Case two now, and a jump from fluid to genetics. This one's in Kidney Medicine.

Most of us think of polycystic kidney disease as a PKD1 or PKD2 story. GANAB is a much rarer cause, and because so few cases have been published, the full range of what it looks like is still being worked out. Cysts in the kidneys and the liver are well described. Skin findings are not part of the recognised picture at all.

The patient was a woman in her late fifties, and what stood out first was her skin. She had multiple cafe-au-lait macules, those flat coffee-coloured patches, together with freckling in the armpits. If you have seen neurofibromatosis type one, that combination is exactly the pattern you would expect to find.

(Exact values: 59-year-old woman.)

Now to the testing.

The obvious test came first. They looked at the NF1 gene and at SPRED1, the gene behind the Legius syndrome look-alike, and both were negative. Only when they moved to genome sequencing did they find the answer, and it was somewhere else entirely: a single pathogenic change in GANAB that was present in the germline, in one copy, and stops the protein short.

(Exact values: Heterozygous pathogenic germline variant GANAB c.181C>T, p.Arg61*.)

And then the kidneys.

The imaging then filled in the kidney side of the story. An MRI showed numerous cysts in the liver and multiple cysts in both kidneys, which fits what has been described with this gene.

Here's where the authors do something I want to point at.

The authors raise the possibility that the picture of GANAB-related disease may turn out to include these pigmented patches and armpit freckles. They are unusually blunt about how far that can be taken. One person is not evidence of an association, it could easily be coincidence, and they describe their own observation as a hypothesis to be tested by collecting more cases.

What the abstract leaves out matters too.

The abstract offers no biological mechanism that would connect this gene to pigmented skin patches, and it does not tell us whether anyone else in the family carries the variant or has the same skin findings, which is the observation that would make the link convincing. We also do not learn her kidney function, how large or how numerous the cysts were, or what happened to her afterwards.

Case three takes us into transplant, and it's also from Kidney Medicine.

Opportunistic infection is one of the commonest problems after a solid organ transplant, and in the kidney it creates a particular trap. A virus attacking the graft and the body rejecting the graft can look very similar on the biopsy slide, and the differences can be subtle. Getting it wrong matters, because, as the authors put it, the treatment strategies for the two are fundamentally contradictory.

Donor-derived cell-free DNA is DNA from the donated kidney that can be measured in the recipient's blood. As a test it has been validated for picking up rejection, both cellular and antibody-mediated. What it means when the graft is infected rather than rejected is much less settled, and the authors point out that almost all of that experience comes from BK polyomavirus nephropathy.

So, this patient.

The patient developed acute kidney injury not long after the transplant. The donor-derived cell-free DNA level was up, which on its own would make most of us think about rejection. The biopsy said something different: the changes were characteristic of adenovirus infecting the graft.

And here the abstract runs out.

The title promises serial monitoring, but the abstract never gives us a single number. We do not see the donor-derived cell-free DNA values, we do not learn how far from the transplant this happened, and we are not told what treatment was given or whether the graft was saved. Most importantly for the question being asked, the abstract does not show that the biomarker itself could separate the virus from rejection, and that is the piece the full text would need to supply.

Now the case I've read in full, from Clinical Kidney Journal. This is the one where we can follow how the team got there.

Start with the background. It's what makes the rest make sense.

The tamper-resistant version of long-acting oxycodone gets its hardness from a polymer called polyethylene oxide. Swallowed as a tablet, that polymer is regarded as harmless because the gut does not absorb it. But when the tablet is crushed and injected into a vein, the polymer goes straight into the bloodstream, and the authors describe animal experiments in which injecting it caused reversible destruction of red cells and a fall in platelets in a dose-related way. The same polymer sits in another long-acting opioid product that has been tied to similar blood vessel injury.

Now the patient, and how she arrived.

The patient was a woman in her mid-thirties with no medical history worth mentioning and no regular medicines. She came in after about a week of feeling unwell, with muscle aches, vomiting, and urine output that was dropping away. Right at the start of the illness she had one episode that looked like a seizure, but nothing focal afterwards. On arrival she was awake, not feverish, and her blood pressure and pulse were normal, although she looked dry. There were no needle marks and nothing on the skin to suggest endocarditis. She did say she had injected an illicit drug, but she could not say what it was.

(Exact values: 35-year-old Aboriginal Australian woman; 6-day history of malaise, myalgia, vomiting and reduced urine output; 1 episode of seizure-like activity at illness onset; blood pressure 118/75 mmHg, pulse 90 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, oxygen saturation 99% on room air.)

From the presentation to the workup.

Her blood film told the story quickly: fragmented red cells in moderate numbers, the picture of red cells being sheared apart inside small vessels. Her white cell count was normal, and apart from a raised fibrinogen her clotting tests were normal. The urine protein-to-creatinine ratio was several times above the upper limit of normal, so she was losing a lot of protein. A wide screen for autoimmune disease, infection, paraprotein and complement problems came back clean, and scans of her abdomen, pelvis and brain showed nothing important.

(Exact values: Fibrinogen 6.0 g/l (reference range 1.7-4.5 g/l); Random urine protein-to-creatinine ratio 110 g/mol (reference range <15 g/mol); Urine microscopy: erythrocytes 10 x 10^6/l, leucocytes 20 x 10^6/l, epithelial cells 10 x 10^6/l, no bacterial growth.)

So, what did the team think they were dealing with?

She was admitted with severe kidney injury from a thrombotic microangiopathy of unclear cause. Because of that seizure-like episode, and because her PLASMIC score sat in the intermediate band, the team could not rule out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, where delay is dangerous. Within a day of arriving she stopped passing urine altogether. She was given fresh frozen plasma, started on plasma exchange, and started on haemodialysis, all while the ADAMTS13 result was still pending.

(Exact values: PLASMIC score 5 points (intermediate); Anuric within 24 hours of presentation; 2 units of fresh frozen plasma.)

And here's the turn.

The diagnosis turned on a second conversation. When she was asked directly, she remembered crushing and injecting long-acting oxycodone with her partner just before she became unwell, and said she had been doing this for a long time, injecting a large dose each time. That made the injected opioid the most likely trigger, and plasma exchange was stopped after only one session, with the ADAMTS13 still pending. Her ADAMTS13 activity then came back squarely in the normal range, which took thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura off the table.

(Exact values: Typically injecting up to 120 mg of modified-release oxycodone at a time; ADAMTS13 activity 94% (reference range 40%-130%); Therapeutic plasma exchange discontinued after 1 session.)

From the diagnosis to the outcome.

In the end she needed very little dialysis: one eight-hour run of continuous haemodiafiltration in intensive care, then a single session of ordinary haemodialysis on the ward. In hospital she had no access to the drug. Her urine output picked up and her creatinine fell steadily. A kidney biopsy was discussed but held back, because she was so clearly getting better, and anti-complement treatment was judged unnecessary. She went home after twelve days with a much lower creatinine, a falling LDH, a rising haemoglobin and a platelet count back to normal.

(Exact values: One 8-hour session of continuous venovenous haemodiafiltration, then one session of intermittent haemodialysis; 12-day hospital stay; Serum creatinine at discharge 209 micromol/l; Lactate dehydrogenase at discharge 258 U/l.)

Then the follow-up, such as it is.

She never came back to the kidney clinic. The follow-up data exist only because she turned up in the emergency department about six months later with back pain. By then her creatinine was normal and her LDH was normal too, which the authors take as complete recovery of both her kidneys and her blood counts after an episode that had needed dialysis.

(Exact values: Re-presented to the emergency department 6 months later; Serum creatinine 54 micromol/l at 6 months; Lactate dehydrogenase 198 U/l at 6 months.)

Let's stop narrating and appraise it. What do the authors make of their own case?

The authors present this as the first report of this particular pattern: oxycodone-related thrombotic microangiopathy severe enough to cause anuria and require dialysis, and then a full recovery once the drug was out of the picture. Earlier published cases had only mild or moderate kidney injury. They accept that plasma and one session of plasma exchange were given, but argue from the timing that stopping the exposure was what mattered most. They also place the case in context: drug-induced disease accounts for something like one in ten to one in eight cases of thrombotic microangiopathy, and the published approach to it rests on removing the offending agent and giving supportive care.

(Exact values: Drug-induced TMA represents 10%-13% of all TMA cases (cited literature).)

And now the honest part.

There are real limits to what this case can prove. No kidney biopsy was done, so the diagnosis rests on blood tests and the clinical picture rather than on tissue, and other causes were never formally excluded. The urine screen detected opiates but was never tested specifically for oxycodone, so the exposure itself is supported by her own account rather than by a laboratory measurement. She also received plasma and one session of plasma exchange before the drug history came out, so those treatments cannot be completely separated from her recovery. And she was not followed in clinic, so the good outcome was documented only because she happened to return to hospital for something else.

Before we get to today's words and phrases, let's pull the main discussion together.

Different patients, different journals, and the same shape repeating. Each time, the first explanation held until somebody looked again.

And the same limit sits under all of today's cases. A single case report cannot establish cause, and it cannot promise that what worked here will work in the next patient.

What a report like this can do is widen the list of things you think of, and stay honest about what it hasn't shown.
