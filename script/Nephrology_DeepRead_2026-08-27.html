Here's a contrast worth holding onto before we start. Ask two nephrologists what a kidney disease actually is, and you can get two very different answers.

The first answer is about the organ. What the biopsy shows, what the urine shows, and how quickly the damage is moving.

The second answer sits upstream. It's about the immune system that keeps feeding the damage, somewhere well away from the kidney itself.

Neither answer is wrong. But whichever one you carry into clinic quietly decides what you think a drug is for.

And it decides what you expect a trial to measure. Treat the organ, and you watch the organ. Aim upstream, and you have to ask whether the organ ever notices.

That question sits underneath today's paper, so keep it somewhere nearby.

So, a single paper today, and I have the verified open-access full text in front of me, not just the abstract.

It's in Clinical Kidney Journal, published online in July of this year, and the first author works in the Renal Division at Peking University First Hospital in Beijing.

It earns the full twenty minutes on the strength of its methods, not its headline. Everything you're allowed to conclude here is decided by how the analysis was built.

Now, let's get started.

Let me give you the shape of it first. Then we'll take apart the methods, because that's where the real argument lives.

IgA nephropathy is the most common primary glomerulonephritis in the world, and China carries an especially heavy share of it. The introduction reports that it accounts for somewhere between a third and a half of primary glomerular disease there, and that even with the supportive care we consider standard, as many as a quarter of Chinese patients reach kidney failure within six years. The drug in question, sibeprenlimab, is a humanized monoclonal antibody that blocks APRIL, a cytokine that pushes B cells to make the galactose-deficient IgA1 sitting at the center of this disease. The global interim analysis of the same trial had already shown roughly a fifty percent placebo-adjusted drop in proteinuria at nine months, and that result supported an accelerated approval in the United States in late twenty twenty-five.

(Exact values: share of primary glomerular disease in China 30%-53%; seen on up to 40% of biopsies there; kidney failure by 6 years in up to 24% despite current care; usual age at diagnosis 20-40 years; sibeprenlimab is a humanized IgG2 anti-APRIL monoclonal antibody; global VISIONARY interim, placebo-adjusted 24-hour uPCR reduction 51.2% at 9 months, P < .0001; FDA accelerated approval 25 November 2025.)

Notice what that background is doing. It sets up a heavy burden, and then it points the treatment away from the kidney and towards the signal feeding it.

And notice the currency it trades in. Protein in the urine. Not survival, not dialysis, not years of preserved function.

That's the question the whole field is waiting on, and this paper can't answer it. What it can do is ask something narrower.

Does an effect seen across a global trial turn up again in a single country's patients, in that country's hospitals, under that country's usual care?

That sounds like a small question. It isn't. Regional cohorts are where a lot of promising effects quietly shrink.

Now to the methods, because that is where the limits of the whole exercise get set.

The parent study, VISIONARY, is a double-blind, placebo-controlled phase three trial running at two hundred forty sites in thirty-one countries, twenty-five of them in China. Adults with biopsy-proven IgA nephropathy were assigned one to one to sibeprenlimab four hundred milligrams or placebo, given under the skin every four weeks for up to twenty-six doses across about two years. To get in, patients needed proteinuria at or above the trial threshold, an estimated GFR of at least thirty, and at least three months on the highest tolerated dose of an ACE inhibitor or an ARB, so everyone was already on optimized supportive care, and a stable SGLT2 inhibitor was allowed if it had been started at least three months before screening. The primary endpoint is the change in twenty-four-hour urine protein-to-creatinine ratio at nine months. The endpoint most of us actually care about, the annualized eGFR slope over twenty-four months, is still blinded and is not reported in this paper.

(Exact values: sibeprenlimab 400 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks, up to 26 doses over up to 100 weeks; randomization 1:1; 240 sites in 31 countries, 25 sites in China; entry 24-hour uPCR >=0.75 g/g or 24-hour urine protein >=1.0 g/day; screening eGFR >=30 ml/min/1.73 m2; stable maximally tolerated ACE inhibitor and/or ARB for >=3 months; primary endpoint change from baseline in 24-hour uPCR at 9 months; key secondary endpoint annualized eGFR slope over 24 months, still blinded.)

A couple of things in there are worth pulling out. The first is what the patients were already taking.

This wasn't a trial of a drug against nothing. Each patient arrived on optimised background treatment, so whatever the drug did, it did on top of that.

The second is the endpoint. The question this analysis can reach is about protein in the urine at a fixed point in time.

The measure that would actually settle the matter is still sealed. It stays sealed until the trial finishes, and that includes us.

This is where the methods really matter. One hundred two patients were randomized in mainland China, but only forty-two of them belonged to the prespecified global analysis population, and those forty-two, twenty-five on sibeprenlimab and seventeen on placebo, are the entire efficacy analysis. The remaining sixty came in through an extended China enrolment program set up for regional representation, and they were left out of the efficacy comparison, though safety and pharmacodynamics did use everyone randomized. The authors are refreshingly explicit about what that costs them. No sample size calculation was done for this regional interim look. The randomization stratification factors were dropped from the model. There was no type one error control for efficacy, which is why you will not see a p-value attached to the China result. Missing nine-month values were filled in by multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption, and no intercurrent event analyses were run because the sample was too small. The primary endpoint was analyzed with analysis of covariance, the spot ratio with a mixed model for repeated measures, and remission with logistic regression.

(Exact values: 156 screened in mainland China, 102 randomized, 54 screening failures; randomized population 42 from the main global population plus 60 from an extended China enrolment program; China interim efficacy analysis set 42 (sibeprenlimab n=25, placebo n=17); safety and pharmacodynamic population N=102; 3 of the 42 discontinued treatment; global interim cohort n=320, the first 62.5% randomized of the main global population N=510; data cutoffs global 4 September 2024 and China 3 January 2025.)

That's a dense passage, so let me pull out what it costs you.

A randomised trial protects you with three things. Randomisation itself, an analysis written down in advance, and a fixed error rate that stops chance findings being read as real.

This regional analysis keeps the first and gives up part of the other two. So what you're reading is a look, not a test.

That isn't a criticism of the authors. They chose to say it plainly rather than dress it up, which is exactly what you want in an interim regional report.

Methods takeaway, then. Read what follows as a description of what happened in this group. Don't read it as a verdict.

Now the results. I'll keep the exact figures on the page rather than in your ear.

At nine months, twenty-four-hour urine protein-to-creatinine ratio fell by about sixty-three percent from baseline on sibeprenlimab, while the placebo group barely moved, down about three percent. That works out to a placebo-adjusted reduction of roughly sixty-two percent, with a ninety-five percent confidence interval running from the low forties up to the mid seventies. For comparison, the global interim cohort came in around fifty-one percent. So the China number is numerically larger, but look at the width of that interval, which is exactly what forty-two patients buys you. The spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio told the same story, separating from placebo by week four and holding through month twelve, and at twelve months the placebo-adjusted reduction in the twenty-four-hour measure was still around fifty-one percent, though the placebo arm itself had drifted down by then.

(Exact values: 9 months, sibeprenlimab -63.1% from baseline in 24-hour uPCR, 95% CI -52.1% to -71.6%; placebo -3.1%, 95% CI -31.0% to 28.3%; placebo-adjusted reduction 61.9%, 95% CI 43.2% to 74.5%; global interim comparator 51.2%, 96.5% CI 42.9% to 58.2%, P < .0001; 12 months placebo-adjusted reduction 51.5%, 95% CI 23.1% to 69.4%; month 9 spot uPCR placebo-adjusted reduction 60.2%, 95% CI 40.3% to 73.4%; no P value is reported for the China placebo-adjusted result.)

Let me give you the generous reading, and then the careful reading.

The generous reading is that the effect turned up again, in a different health system, and it wasn't a whisper.

The careful reading is about that interval. When a range that wide is the honest summary of what you know, the point estimate sitting in the middle is the least interesting part of it.

So the sentence I'd write down is that the China cohort is consistent with the larger trial. Not better than it. Consistent with it.

Two downstream signals in this China cohort point in the same direction. Proteinuric remission, defined as urine protein below half a gram a day at twelve months, was reached by six of fourteen patients on sibeprenlimab and one of eleven on placebo. Dipstick blood in the urine, present in the large majority of both arms at baseline, was down to four of eighteen on sibeprenlimab at week forty-eight but was still there in twelve of fourteen placebo patients. Here is the thing worth flagging out loud: those denominators are much smaller than the forty-two patients in the efficacy set, and the paper never says why, so the honest way to quote these is with the denominator attached, not as a rate across the whole cohort. On the biology side, sibeprenlimab shut down free APRIL almost completely by week forty-eight and cut galactose-deficient IgA1 by roughly two thirds, with serum IgA, IgG and IgM all falling as well, while placebo barely moved.

(Exact values: proteinuric remission at 12 months 6/14, 42.9%, 95% CI 17.7% to 71.1% with sibeprenlimab versus 1/11, 9.1%, 95% CI 0.2% to 41.3% with placebo; baseline dipstick haematuria 20/25 and 16/17; week 48 dipstick haematuria 4/18, 22.2%, versus 12/14, 85.7%; galactose-deficient IgA1 mean reduction 67.3%, SD 10.2, at week 48; APRIL mean reduction 97.7%, SD 0.8, at week 48; week 48 IgA 67.1%, IgG 32.0%, IgM 74.4%; the source does not reconcile the smaller denominators against the 42-patient efficacy set.)

The denominator problem there is worth naming twice, because it's exactly the sort of thing that gets lost when a result is quoted second hand.

Quote those proportions with the group they came from attached, or don't quote them at all.

The biology is the cleanest part of the paper. Block a signal, and the things that signal drives fall away. At the very least, the drug is doing what it says on the label.

That isn't the same as helping the patient. It's a reason to keep looking.

On safety, across all one hundred two randomized patients in China, treatment-emergent adverse events were reported in about seventy-nine percent on sibeprenlimab and about seventy-three percent on placebo. Serious events were uncommon in both arms, none of them judged treatment related in the sibeprenlimab group, and there were no deaths. The one clear difference was infections, roughly fifty-one percent versus twenty-nine percent, driven mainly by upper respiratory tract infections that were mostly mild and cleared without any change in dosing, with no opportunistic infections such as herpes or candidiasis reported. Injection site reactions were mild and a little more common with the active drug, mostly injection site pain. And notably, given that this drug suppresses immunoglobulins, no sibeprenlimab patient fell below an IgG of four hundred milligrams per deciliter after baseline.

(Exact values: safety population N=102, sibeprenlimab 47 and placebo 55; treatment-emergent adverse events 78.7% versus 72.7%; serious events 4.3%, none treatment related, versus 5.5%, no deaths; infections and infestations 51.1% versus 29.1%; upper respiratory tract infection 29.8% versus 18.2%, mild in 23.4% of sibeprenlimab patients; injection site reactions 5/47, 10.6%, versus 3/55, 5.5%; no post-baseline IgG below 400 mg/dl in the sibeprenlimab group.)

A safety picture this size can only rule out the obvious. Rare harm needs thousands of patients and years of follow-up, and this has neither.

What it can give you is the texture of what did show up, and the texture here is unremarkable rather than reassuring. Those are different words on purpose.

Now to the part that decides how much of all this you carry out of the room.

The limitations deserve as much airtime as the results here. This is an interim look at a trial that is still running, and the endpoint is proteinuria, a surrogate. The endpoint that will actually tell us whether kidney function is preserved, the annualized eGFR slope over twenty-four months, is still blinded and is not in this paper. Haematuria was judged by dipstick, a semiquantitative test, because urine microscopy was only available in some patients. There was no sample size calculation, no error rate control, and only forty-two patients in the efficacy set, which is why the authors call these early indicators of a treatment effect rather than confirmation. There was also a sex imbalance between the arms, with more women on active drug, which the authors attribute to chance in a small subgroup where sex was not a stratification factor. And the comparisons in the discussion with other IgA nephropathy drugs are explicitly descriptive, because the trials differ in design, population and endpoint, so nothing here ranks one drug against another.

(Exact values: interim data from an ongoing trial with proteinuria at 9 months as a surrogate endpoint; annualized eGFR slope over 24 months remains blinded and unreported; haematuria assessed by dipstick at week 48, with urine microscopy available only in a subset; no formal sample size calculation, stratification factors removed, no type 1 error control, and no supplementary, sensitivity or subgroup analyses of the primary endpoint; efficacy analysis set limited to 42 patients; 52.4% female overall, and sex was not a stratification factor.)

Those are the authors' own limits, and they set them honestly. Let me add a couple of mine, clearly labelled as mine.

The first is about reach. This describes the patients who were enrolled, and that's the limit of it. Not the person with advanced disease, not a child, not somebody whose diagnosis was left unconfirmed under the microscope.

The second is about what a surrogate can buy you. A urine measurement that moves is a reason to keep going. It is not yet a reason to promise anything about the years ahead.

So where does that leave the drug in practice? Honestly, close to where the authors leave it.

The signal repeated in a population that carries a great deal of this disease, and it repeated in the direction you would want. That is genuinely worth something.

What it does not do is rank this drug against the others arriving in the same space. Different trials, different patients, different endpoints, and nothing compared side by side.

And the thing that would move practice is the thing still under seal. Until that opens, this is evidence about a marker, and the authors say so themselves.
