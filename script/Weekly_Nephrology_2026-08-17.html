# Weekly Nephrology AI - Weekly Summary - 2026-08-17

## Script

Welcome to Weekly Nephrology AI. This episode is published on August 17, 2026.

This podcast is generated by AI and may contain errors. Before using this information in clinical practice or research, please review the full text of the original paper.

This is an educational podcast about recent nephrology research, with clear English and careful evidence review.

I'm Emma. I'll walk you through each study, explain the evidence in clear English, and summarize what matters.

Today's series is Weekly Summary. We review five recent clinical studies using their abstracts.

Before we get into today's episode, let's ease in with a little Small Talk connected to today's topic.

Picture the last person you saw who was in their twenties, felt completely well, and was only in the building for a work medical.

Would you have sent a urine sample with the blood test? Most of us would not.

Albuminuria and estimated GFR are the two markers we lean on to pick up chronic kidney disease before anyone feels unwell, and yet pairing them in preventive screening, particularly in young adults, is still not routine.

That gap, between what we are able to measure and what we actually order, is where today's first study begins.

A word on what today is built from. Everything comes from published abstracts rather than the full papers, so I will stay inside what each abstract actually reports.

I will name the journal for each one. Author affiliations were mostly not available to me, so I will leave them out unless the abstract itself names the setting.

What these five studies share is simple. Each sits close to a decision somebody makes in an ordinary clinic week.

Now, let's get started.

We start in Clinical Kidney Journal, published this month, with the screening programme I just mentioned.

The investigators screened just over twelve thousand hospital staff, from their late teens to their early seventies, over a four-year period, measuring both an estimated GFR and a spot urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio in everyone and then grading them with the KDIGO categories.

(Exact values: 12 281 healthcare workers aged 19-71 years, screened 2021-2024, University Hospital of Bari, Italy; eGFR by CKD-EPI; uACR from spot urine; classification by KDIGO 2024 criteria; Age strata used: 19-30, 31-60 and >60 years.)

Here is what the two tests turned up together.

Across this whole workforce of hospital staff, close to seven percent met the KDIGO definition of chronic kidney disease, and almost all of that came from raised albuminuria rather than a low filtration rate, because only about one in five hundred had an estimated GFR under sixty.

(Exact values: 6.7% of the 12 281 screened workers met KDIGO criteria for CKD; 6.5% had elevated uACR (>30 mg/g); 0.2% had reduced eGFR (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2).)

Now the youngest group, which is the part that made this study worth choosing.

Among the youngest workers, those between nineteen and thirty, roughly ninety-five percent had a completely normal kidney profile, but close to one in twenty already had raised albuminuria even though their filtration rate looked fine.

(Exact values: Young adults aged 19-30 years (n = 4139): 95.3% G1-G2/A1; 4.7% A2-A3 with preserved eGFR; Age strata as printed: 19-30 n = 4139; 31-60 n = 5245; >60 n = 2907 (sum 12 291) versus stated total of 12 281 participants.)

Before we leave it, the limits.

It is worth remembering what this design can and cannot tell us. This was a one-off screening snapshot of a working, largely healthy population at a single Italian hospital, so it describes how common these findings are rather than what happens to people afterwards, and the age-group counts printed in the abstract do not add up neatly to the stated total.

Second study, and it moves from a whole workforce to just twenty patients with a rare kidney disease. It is a randomised trial, published in June in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation.

Proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits is rare enough that almost nobody has run a controlled trial in it. Clinicians increasingly aim treatment at the underlying clone, either the plasma cells or the B cells, but until now there has been no prospective comparison of those two strategies.

So what did they do?

Twenty patients were randomly assigned in equal numbers, one arm to a bortezomib-based plasma-cell regimen and the other to rituximab, and the trial's main endpoint was overall kidney response at twelve months.

(Exact values: 20 patients randomised 1:1 (BCD n = 10; rituximab n = 10); BCD given for 6 cycles; rituximab 375 mg/m2 weekly for 4 doses; Prespecified rituximab booster 500 mg at 6 months if B-cell reconstitution >5/uL; Primary endpoint: overall renal response at 12 months.)

Let's slow down for the main result, because the wording matters more than the figures.

In the intention-to-treat analysis of all twenty randomised patients, roughly six in ten reached an overall kidney response on the bortezomib regimen and roughly seven in ten on rituximab. With ten patients in each arm, that gap is well inside the play of chance, so it cannot tell us which regimen is better.

(Exact values: Overall renal response at 12 months: BCD 60% (6/10) vs rituximab 70% (7/10), P = 1.0; Intention-to-treat population n = 20.)

Around that headline, two smaller observations.

Across both arms of these twenty patients, protein leak fell and serum albumin rose over the year. The bortezomib arm showed a temporary dip in filtration rate at the halfway point, and that dip had recovered by twelve months.

(Exact values: Both groups: reduction in proteinuria and improvement in serum albumin over 12 months; Transient eGFR decline in the BCD group at 6 months, recovered by 12 months; Complete response at 6 months: 5 patients (BCD) vs 3 patients (rituximab).)

And safety.

Safety in these twenty patients looked manageable overall. There was a single serious adverse event, a pneumonia, and it happened in the bortezomib arm, which is also where the two treatment failures occurred, against none on rituximab.

(Exact values: 1 serious adverse event (pneumonia) in the BCD group; Treatment failure: 2 patients (BCD) vs 0 patients (rituximab).)

Now the authors' own reading of it.

The authors are careful about how far this goes. It was a small, open-label pilot at one centre, and the two arms differed in how the treatment was structured, so they present the results as a starting point for larger multicentre trials rather than an answer. A similar response rate in a trial this size is not the same as showing the two regimens are equally good.

Our third paper is not a study at all. It is a review with a proposal attached, written by a European working group, in the same journal.

Fracture risk climbs steadily as kidney disease advances, and by the most advanced stages, dialysis included, it sits several times higher than in people of the same age and sex whose kidneys are healthy.

There is a name for that problem now.

In twenty twenty-three, KDIGO introduced the label CKD-associated osteoporosis. The point of the name is that the work-up and the drug choices overlap heavily with osteoporosis in anyone else, while the plan still has to be individualised around the kidney disease.

(Exact values: KDIGO introduced the term 'CKD-associated osteoporosis' in 2023.)

Which raises the obvious question. How solid is the evidence sitting under the label?

The authors are blunt about the evidence gap. The strategies we accept for postmenopausal women and older men rest on guideline-level evidence, but whether those same treatments help in advanced kidney disease has never been settled by randomised trials.

So what do the authors suggest doing about it?

The authors set out a practical order of play. Lifestyle measures should be on the table for everyone, since the risk is low and the potential return is high, and mineral metabolism should be sorted out first, including therapy aimed at parathyroid hormone, before any bone-targeting drug is started.

Before we move on.

One caveat matters here. This is a review and an expert proposal, not new trial data, and the authors say themselves that the definitive trials in advanced kidney disease have not been done. No fracture-reduction numbers for any individual drug are given.

Fourth, and we cross to the Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, also published this month.

The question here is what changes when a glomerular disease begins late in life.

The investigators took more than fifteen hundred adults with biopsy-proven primary glomerular disease from the CureGN cohort and split them by age at diagnosis into three bands, with a little over two hundred patients who were sixty-five or older.

(Exact values: 1562 adults enrolled in CureGN between 2014 and 2023; Age bands at diagnosis: 18-39, 40-64 and >=65 years; 211 patients aged >=65 years at diagnosis; counts for the 18-39 and 40-64 bands not reported in the abstract.)

First, how these older patients looked at the start.

Among these patients, the ones diagnosed at sixty-five or older arrived with more comorbidity and worse kidney function, with a median filtration rate in the mid-fifties against the eighties in the youngest band.

(Exact values: Median eGFR at enrolment: >=65 years 55 (IQR 34-75); 18-39 years 84 (50-115); 40-64 years 65 (43-91) mL/min; P<0.001.)

Then the biopsies.

In the subset of biopsies with the study's pathology scoring available, the background nephrosclerosis, and arteriosclerosis in particular, became more prominent with age, while the features that define each glomerular disease looked much the same in every age band.

(Exact values: 704 of 1562 biopsies had an available Cure Glomerulopathy Pathology Classification.)

Now treatment and survival.

Older patients in this cohort were more often managed conservatively or on steroid-sparing regimens, and over five years they carried an excess mortality of somewhere between seven and fourteen percent compared with the youngest group.

(Exact values: 5-year excess mortality of 7-14% in patients aged >=65 years versus young adults aged 18-39 years.)

And here is the finding that catches people out.

In the adjusted models, older age at diagnosis was independently associated with a lower risk of the composite kidney outcome, meaning a large fall in filtration rate or kidney failure. That is an association in an observational cohort, and it sits alongside the higher mortality these older patients experienced.

(Exact values: Composite kidney outcome defined as >40% eGFR decline or kidney failure; Adjusted subdistribution hazard ratio for older age: 0.65-0.79.)

That one needs care in how we read it.

A few caveats keep this in proportion. It is a retrospective look at an observational cohort, so we are talking about associations rather than cause and effect, the pathology scoring was only available for a subset of the biopsies, and the seemingly lower kidney risk in older patients cannot be untangled from the fact that more of them died during follow-up.

Last one, and on the surface it sits furthest from a kidney clinic. It comes from The Lancet Global Health.

People living with HIV often have a hard time getting care for their other chronic conditions, even though that disease burden is growing and so is the mortality that comes with it.

Here is how they set it up.

Fourteen HIV clinics in Botswana were paired off, and one clinic from each pair was randomly assigned to fold hypertension care into the existing HIV visit, with staff training, ongoing coaching, community treatment partners and the national electronic record doing the heavy lifting. About four and a half thousand adults with both conditions took part, split evenly between the two arms.

(Exact values: 14 HIV clinics, pair-matched; one clinic per pair randomly allocated to intervention or standard of care; 4654 participants enrolled (2327 per group), aged 20-75 years, between Jan 13, 2023 and Sept 10, 2025; Blood pressure targets: <140 mm Hg systolic and <90 mm Hg diastolic; <130/80 mm Hg for diabetes or chronic kidney disease; Co-primary outcomes assessed at 12 months; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05414526.)

Take the first of the two main outcomes.

Among the participants who were actually on blood-pressure medicines, about two thirds in the integrated-care clinics were at target after a year, against about half in the clinics carrying on as usual.

(Exact values: Blood pressure control at 12 months among participants on antihypertensives: 719/1072 (67.1%) intervention vs 593/1152 (51.5%) standard of care; Risk ratio 1.29, 95% CI 1.10-1.51; p=0.0013 (unadjusted).)

Now the second of the two.

Electronic prescribing of blood-pressure medicines shifted even more dramatically, from roughly one in ten eligible encounters under standard care to roughly four in ten in the integrated-care clinics, although the confidence interval around that ratio is wide.

(Exact values: Electronic antihypertensive prescription: 428/1072 (39.9%) intervention vs 124/1152 (10.8%) standard of care; Risk ratio 5.95, 95% CI 2.18-16.2; p=0.0005 (unadjusted).)

Two other things the trial kept an eye on.

Serious adverse events were uncommon and looked much the same in both arms, and viral suppression stayed above ninety-eight percent on either side with no statistically significant difference detected. In other words, the trial found no signal that the extra hypertension work came at the expense of HIV care.

(Exact values: Serious adverse events at 12 months: 22/2127 (0.9%) intervention vs 20/2188 (0.9%); p=0.69; Viral suppression: 1759/1784 (98.6%) intervention vs 1821/1839 (99.0%); p=0.39.)

And now what this trial cannot tell us.

Some boundaries are worth naming. Only clinics that already ran the national electronic health record could take part, so this is a particular kind of setting, the headline comparisons are the unadjusted ones, and the denominators for each outcome are subsets of everyone enrolled rather than the whole trial. Similar viral suppression in the two arms also is not proof that the two models of care are equivalent on that measure.
