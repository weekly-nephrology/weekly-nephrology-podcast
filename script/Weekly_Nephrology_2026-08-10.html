I'm Emma. I'll walk you through each study, explain the evidence in clear English, and summarize what matters.

Today's series is Weekly Summary. We review five recent clinical studies using their abstracts.

Before we get into today's episode, let's ease in with a little Small Talk connected to today's topic.

Here is a question I keep coming back to. How well do we know the numbers we treat to?

We write a proteinuria target into the chart, and then we chase it for years.

Guidelines from KDIGO and IPNA set a proteinuria target below 0.2 grams per day per 1.73 square metres in children with IgA nephropathy. Until now, the evidence behind that threshold came from small single-ethnicity cohorts, and the long-term kidney outcomes were unknown.

(Exact values: Guideline proteinuria target in children with IgA nephropathy: <0.2 g/day/1.73 m2 (KDIGO and IPNA).)

So where did that number come from, and does reaching it change anything long term?

A quick word on what you are hearing. Everything today comes from published abstracts, not full papers.

So I will tell you where a result looks firm, and where the abstract does not say more.

I will name the journal for each study. Publication dates and author affiliations were not available.

Each study this week asks whether a treatment, or a target, really protects the kidney, and in whom.

Now, let's get started.

We start in JAMA.

Glomerular diseases are a leading cause of chronic kidney disease and kidney failure. Finerenone, a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, is known to reduce the risk of kidney function loss in chronic kidney disease, but its effect in people whose kidney disease comes from a glomerular disease was uncertain.

So the question is whether that protection can carry over when the damage starts in the glomerulus.

This was a prespecified exploratory subgroup analysis of a phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in adults with nondiabetic chronic kidney disease. Of 1584 participants, 903 had an investigator-reported glomerular disease, and almost half of them had IgA nephropathy. Participants took finerenone 10 or 20 milligrams once daily, or a matching placebo.

(Exact values: 903 of 1584 participants (57.0%) had investigator-reported glomerular disease; IgA nephropathy 416 (46.1%), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis 215 (23.8%), membranous nephropathy 90 (10.0%); Finerenone 10 mg or 20 mg once daily n = 446 vs matching placebo n = 457; Mean eGFR 48.8 mL/min/1.73 m2; median urinary albumin to creatinine ratio 839.6 mg/g; mean age 51.1 (SD 13.6) years; 362 female (40.1%); 558 Asian (61.9%).)

Notice the design word there. This group was carved out of a larger trial, not recruited for one of its own.

First, how fast kidney function fell.

Kidney function declined more slowly with finerenone. The total eGFR slope over 32 months was about minus 3.5 millilitres per minute per 1.73 square metres per year with finerenone, compared with about minus 4.2 with placebo, and the confidence interval for that difference stayed on the side of benefit.

(Exact values: Total eGFR slope to month 32: -3.50 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (finerenone) vs -4.23 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (placebo); Between-group difference 0.73 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI, 0.22-1.24).)

Does a difference that small show up in outcomes patients can feel?

For the harder outcome of kidney failure or a sustained forty percent or greater fall in eGFR, the rate was about 7.4 events per 100 patient-years with finerenone and about 9.6 with placebo, giving a hazard ratio of 0.74.

(Exact values: Kidney failure or sustained eGFR decline of 40% or more: 7.42 vs 9.60 events per 100 patient-years; Hazard ratio 0.74 (95% CI, 0.57-0.97).)

So the slope and the harder outcome point in the same direction.

Before we lean on that, how much weight can this analysis carry?

One caution here. This was a prespecified exploratory subgroup analysis, the glomerular disease diagnosis was reported by the local investigator rather than centrally confirmed, and the albuminuria and kidney composite results were exploratory outcomes.

A promising direction, then. Not the same thing as a trial designed for glomerular disease from the start.

The next study asks its own question, in a different group of patients.

Lupus nephritis is still a major cause of chronic kidney disease and end-stage kidney disease, even with modern immunosuppression. SGLT2 inhibitors protect the kidney in diabetic and nondiabetic chronic kidney disease, but their renal and blood effects in immune-mediated glomerular diseases like lupus nephritis were not well defined.

Lupus nephritis is exactly where you would want that question settled.

In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 79 adults with biopsy-proven lupus nephritis and an eGFR above 30 received either dapagliflozin 10 milligrams a day or placebo for 12 months, on top of standard immunosuppressive therapy. The primary renal endpoint was the percentage change in 24-hour urinary protein excretion.

(Exact values: 79 adults randomized: dapagliflozin 10 mg/day n = 38, placebo n = 41; Inclusion: biopsy-proven lupus nephritis, eGFR >30 mL/min/1.73 m2; Treatment duration 12 months; primary renal endpoint percentage change in 24-h urinary protein excretion; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05748925.)

Both groups stayed on their usual immunosuppression. The trial is in Clinical Kidney Journal.

At 12 months, proteinuria was numerically lower with dapagliflozin than with placebo, but the difference did not reach statistical significance, and the adjusted analysis confirmed there was no significant treatment effect.

(Exact values: Primary renal endpoint at 12 months: numerical reduction in proteinuria with dapagliflozin versus placebo that did not reach statistical significance; ANCOVA-adjusted analysis: no significant treatment effect; effect size, 95% CI, and p-value not_reported.)

And the kidney function and blood measures?

The trial also looked at kidney function and at blood parameters, including haemoglobin, erythropoietin, hepcidin, ferritin, and transferrin saturation. It did not find statistically significant differences between the groups in eGFR, in eGFR slope, or in those blood parameters.

(Exact values: Haematologic parameters assessed at baseline and 12 months: hemoglobin, erythropoietin, hepcidin, ferritin, transferrin saturation; No significant differences in eGFR, eGFR slope, or haematologic parameters; numeric values, 95% CIs, and p-values not_reported.)

Now, be careful with how you read a result like that.

Keep the size in mind. This was a single trial of 79 patients over 12 months, the authors themselves ask for larger and adequately powered studies, and the abstract gives no effect sizes, confidence intervals, or p-values. The trial was also registered retrospectively.

(Exact values: Sample size 79 patients (dapagliflozin 38, placebo 41); follow-up 12 months; Effect estimates, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values: not_reported in the abstract; Registration NCT05748925, registered 28 February 2023, retrospectively registered.)

The authors describe this as a possible signal rather than an answer, and they say it needs confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies that run for longer.

So, not a green light, and not a closed door either.

From a small trial to a very large one.

This next paper is in Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology.

This was a prespecified pooled analysis of participant-level data from three randomised placebo-controlled trials of semaglutide: SELECT, FLOW, and SOUL. The people enrolled were not the same in each one. FLOW recruited participants who had chronic kidney disease, while SELECT and SOUL recruited participants who had atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and across the three trials participants both with and without diabetes were included. Together they contributed just over 30 thousand participants, with mean follow-up of roughly three to four years. Semaglutide was given either by weekly injection or as a daily tablet.

(Exact values: Pooled N = 30 787 participants from SELECT, FLOW, and SOUL; Mean follow-up 39.5-47.5 months; Semaglutide dosing: once-weekly subcutaneous 1.0 mg (FLOW), once-weekly subcutaneous 2.4 mg (SELECT), once-daily oral 14 mg (SOUL), added to standard of care.)

So this was planned in advance, rather than assembled afterwards from published reports.

The primary outcome was a kidney composite: a persistent fall in eGFR of 50 percent or more, kidney failure, kidney-related death, or cardiovascular death. It is worth noting that cardiovascular death sits inside this primary kidney composite.

(Exact values: Primary composite, time to first event of any of these four components: cardiovascular-related death; kidney-related death; kidney failure, meaning either start of kidney replacement therapy or a persistent eGFR below 15 mL/min per 1.73 m2; and a persistent drop in eGFR of 50% or greater.)

Hold on to that last part when you read the headline result.

Semaglutide reduced the primary kidney composite. There were 973 first events with semaglutide compared with 1134 on placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.84, and the confidence interval stayed below one.

(Exact values: Primary kidney composite first events: 973 (semaglutide) vs 1134 (placebo); Hazard ratio 0.84 (95% CI 0.77-0.91).)

And with cardiovascular death taken out of the count?

The narrower kidney composite, the one that takes cardiovascular death out, moved in the same direction: 347 first events with semaglutide against 416 with placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.80.

(Exact values: Secondary kidney composite excluding cardiovascular-related death: 347 (semaglutide) vs 416 (placebo) first events; Hazard ratio 0.80 (95% CI 0.69-0.92).)

What about harm?

On safety, the picture was reassuring. Across the two groups the safety outcomes looked broadly the same, and they matched what has been seen in other trials of GLP-1 receptor agonists. Serious adverse events were numerically lower on semaglutide than on placebo.

(Exact values: Safety: between-group outcomes overall similar, matching the profile reported for other trials of GLP-1 receptor agonists; Serious adverse events: numerically lower on semaglutide than on placebo across the 3 pooled trials; counts and rates not_reported.)

A couple of things to bear in mind before we move on.

Two caveats. Semaglutide was not given in the same way in all three trials: the dose and the route of administration varied. The people enrolled also differed somewhat at baseline from trial to trial, so this is not one single homogeneous population. And the work was funded by the manufacturer, Novo Nordisk.

Now a change of subject. This next trial, in the Lancet, is not about a kidney drug at all.

It is about which antibiotic you choose for a Staph aureus bloodstream infection, and what that does to the kidney.

This was an open-label, non-inferiority randomised trial run at 67 hospitals across eight countries, inside the SNAP platform trial. Adults admitted with penicillin-susceptible Staph aureus bacteraemia were randomly assigned one to one to benzylpenicillin, or to flucloxacillin or cloxacillin. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 90 days.

(Exact values: Sites: 67 hospitals in 8 countries — Canada, Singapore, Israel and South Africa, plus Australia, New Zealand, the UK and the Netherlands; 1:1 random allocation; adults aged 18 years or older; Primary outcome: all-cause mortality 90 days after platform entry, intention-to-treat; Non-inferiority margin: adjusted odds ratio less than 1.20.)

Survival first.

For the primary outcome, death at 90 days, about 14 percent of the benzylpenicillin group died compared with about 22 percent of the flucloxacillin or cloxacillin group. The adjusted odds ratio was 0.67, and the credible interval still crossed one.

(Exact values: Death at 90 days: 21/152 (14%) benzylpenicillin vs 26/121 (22%) flucloxacillin or cloxacillin; Adjusted OR 0.67 (95% CrI 0.35-1.28); Posterior probability of non-inferiority 96.1%; of superiority 88.9%.)

And here is the part that brings this trial into our clinic.

The kidney signal is what stopped recruitment in this comparison. Acute kidney injury occurred in about 11 percent of the benzylpenicillin group and about 22 percent of the flucloxacillin or cloxacillin group, and the data and safety monitoring committee recommended ceasing recruitment because of that excess acute kidney injury.

(Exact values: AKI: 17/153 (11%) benzylpenicillin vs 27/124 (22%) flucloxacillin or cloxacillin; Adjusted OR 0.50 (95% CrI 0.26-0.94); Posterior probability of non-inferiority 99.8%; of superiority 98.4%; PSSA silo closed for recruitment on Aug 7, 2024, after the fourth interim analysis.)

So does that settle the choice of antibiotic?

The investigators concluded that, taken together, the probability of non-inferiority for mortality and the lower risk of acute kidney injury indicate that benzylpenicillin should be preferred over flucloxacillin or cloxacillin for penicillin-susceptible Staph aureus bacteraemia in adults.

That is the investigators' conclusion, and it comes with conditions.

There are important caveats. Everyone knew which antibiotic was given, because the trial was open-label. Recruitment was ceased before the prespecified stopping thresholds were reached, and the prespecified non-inferiority criterion for benzylpenicillin was not actually met.

(Exact values: Prespecified non-inferiority margin: adjusted odds ratio less than 1.20, not met; Recruitment ceased after the fourth interim analysis, before prespecified stopping thresholds were reached; PSSA silo closed Aug 7, 2024; Registration ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05137119; the SNAP trial is ongoing.)

A real kidney signal, then, inside a trial that did not finish the way it was designed to.

Which brings us back to where we started, with that proteinuria target in children.

This last study is in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation, and it is an observational cohort, not a trial.

The investigators started from a multiethnic cohort of nearly thirteen hundred children with IgA nephropathy and studied the 709 who had proteinuria at biopsy at or above the guideline threshold. These were White, Chinese, and Japanese children, with a median age at biopsy of about 13 years and median proteinuria of 1.2 grams per day per 1.73 square metres. Complete and sustained remission meant remission lasting more than 90 days.

(Exact values: Source cohort 1298 children with IgA nephropathy; 709 analysed with biopsy proteinuria >=0.2 g/day/1.73 m2; Median age at biopsy 12.9 years; median eGFR 95.7 mL/min/1.73 m2; median proteinuria 1.2 g/day/1.73 m2; MEST-C at biopsy: M1 57%, E1 41.3%, S1 53.3%, T1-2 13.6%, C1-2 46.7%; Complete and sustained remission (CSR) defined as remission lasting more than 90 days.)

Remission here is not one good clinic visit. It has to hold.

Just over half the children, 395 of 709, reached complete and sustained remission, and they reached it fairly early, at a time-averaged observation of about 0.7 years. Remission was maintained throughout follow-up in about 47 percent of cases.

(Exact values: CSR in 395 of 709 children (55.7%); Time-averaged observation to CSR 0.7 years (0.4-1.3 years); CSR maintained throughout follow-up in 47.3% of cases.)

And did reaching that target track with better kidney function later on?

In a sensitivity analysis, the children who achieved complete and sustained remission showed significant protection from eGFR decline at four and at five years.

(Exact values: Protection from eGFR decline in children achieving CSR: p = 0.026 at 4 years, p = 0.004 at 5 years.)

What went along with getting there?

About three quarters of the children received renin-angiotensin system blockers during follow-up. In the multivariable model, remission was associated with younger age, higher eGFR, absence of segmental sclerosis on biopsy, and treatment with renin-angiotensin system blockers.

(Exact values: Treatment during follow-up: RAS blockers 74.4%, immunosuppressors 67.4%; Factors associated with CSR: younger age p = 0.009; higher eGFR p = 0.002; absence of segmental sclerosis p < 0.001; RASB treatment p < 0.001; Model including ethnicity: strong effect of Japanese and Chinese ethnicity (both p < 0.001); CSR associated with age, absence of S1 (p < 0.001), absence of T1-2 (p = 0.016), and RASB and IS treatment (both p < 0.01).)

Association, though, is not the same as cause.

The authors are careful with their wording, and we should be too. This is an observational study in children, so it shows that reaching complete sustained remission was associated with a better eGFR outcome. It does not prove that the remission itself caused the better outcome, and the study does not include adults.

So the target we opened with now has a much larger cohort behind it, at least in children.
