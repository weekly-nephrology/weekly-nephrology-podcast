Welcome to Weekly Nephrology AI. This episode is published on August 10, 2026.

This is an educational podcast about recent nephrology research, with clear English and careful evidence review.

I'm Emma. I'll walk you through each study, explain the evidence in clear English, and summarize what matters.

Today's series is Weekly Summary. We review five recent clinical studies using their abstracts.

Before we get into today's episode, let's ease in with a little Small Talk connected to today's topic.

Here's something that comes up whenever a familiar drug meets an unfamiliar disease. We know what it does in one group of patients, and someone asks whether that carries over to another.

Even with everything modern immunosuppression can do, lupus nephritis is still one of the main roads to chronic kidney disease and to kidney failure. We know that SGLT2 inhibitors protect the kidney when the cause is diabetes and when it is not, and that they tend to lift haemoglobin, but nobody has really pinned down what they do in an immune-driven glomerular disease like this one.

That gap, between what has been shown and what has only been hoped for, is where a lot of trials begin.

A word on what today is built from. Everything comes from published abstracts, not the full papers, so I'll stay inside what each abstract reports. I'll name the journal for each one, and leave author affiliations out, because they weren't available to me.

These five sit close to decisions somebody makes in an ordinary week. What the biopsy might show. Whether to add a drug. When to start steroids. Which antibiotic to write up.

Now, let's get started.

We open in Kidney International Reports, with a systematic review.

Antisynthetase syndrome is a rare autoimmune disease defined by autoantibodies that target aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase enzymes, and what it does to the kidney has never been well described. To close that gap, the authors ran a systematic review of four databases, from their earliest records up to the start of this year, looking for adults in whom kidney involvement was either proven on biopsy or clearly defined on clinical grounds. Thirty-eight published articles, covering fifty-two patients between them, met those entry criteria.

(Exact values: Included evidence: 38 articles, 52 adult patients; Databases searched from inception to January 2026: MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar; PRISMA 2020; PROSPERO registration CRD420261282317; risk of bias by JBI tools; certainty by GRADE.)

Here is what came out of it.

In eighty-one percent of these patients the kidney problem showed up alongside interstitial lung disease, or came after it. Thirty-five of them went on to a kidney biopsy, and there the most frequent finding was membranous nephropathy, with pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis close behind.

(Exact values: Kidney disease with or following interstitial lung disease: 81% of cases; Biopsied: 35 of 52 patients; Biopsy lesions: membranous nephropathy 29%, pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis 23%, acute tubular necrosis 17%, tubulointerstitial nephritis 11%.)

Now the antibodies.

Antibodies other than anti-Jo1 dominated among the patients with a vasculitic picture, and those patients climbed to a higher peak creatinine than the anti-Jo1 group, four point seven against two point three milligrams per decilitre.

(Exact values: Peak creatinine, non-Jo1 vs anti-Jo1: 4.7 vs 2.3 mg/dl; P = 0.01.)

And the longer view.

By a median of eighteen months, thirteen percent of the patients had moved on to stage four or stage five chronic kidney disease.

(Exact values: Progression to CKD stages 4-5: 13%, at median follow-up 18 months.)

Now the caution.

Everything here rests on fifty-two patients gathered out of the published literature, not on a prospective cohort, and the authors say so plainly: the link between a non-Jo1 serotype and a worse kidney outcome still has to be confirmed in prospective studies that adjust for several variables at once, and their suggestions about surveillance and antibody-guided treatment are awaiting validation.

Second study, and it takes up exactly that gap. It's a randomised trial in Clinical Kidney Journal.

Seventy-nine adults took part, all of them with lupus nephritis confirmed on biopsy and kidney function above the trial's cut-off. On top of the immunosuppression they were already taking, each one received either dapagliflozin or a matching dummy tablet for a year, and neither the patients nor their doctors knew which. What the researchers measured first was the percentage change in the protein they lost in the urine over a full day.

(Exact values: Randomised: 79 adults; dapagliflozin 10 mg/day n = 38, placebo n = 41; Entry eGFR criterion > 30 ml/min/1.73 m2; treatment duration 12 months; Primary renal endpoint: percentage change in 24-h urinary protein excretion; registration NCT05748925.)

And after a year?

After a year the dapagliflozin group did have a little less protein in the urine than the placebo group, but that difference was not large enough to be statistically significant, and once the analysis was adjusted it still showed no significant treatment effect.

(Exact values: Timepoint 12 months; a numerical reduction in proteinuria with dapagliflozin that remained short of statistical significance after adjustment; effect size, 95% CI and p-value not reported in the abstract.)

So how far do they push it?

The authors are careful about how far they push this. What they describe is a possible signal, nothing more, and they say it needs larger studies, properly powered and running for longer, before anyone draws a conclusion. It is also worth knowing that the trial was entered on the registry after it had already begun, in early two thousand twenty-three.

(Exact values: Authors' framing of the 79-patient, 12-month trial: a possible signal needing confirmation in larger, adequately powered, longer-duration studies; NCT05748925, retrospectively registered 28 February 2023.)

Third, and we move to the Journal of the American Society of Nephrology. This one is a consensus statement, not a study.

Checkpoint inhibitors have rewritten cancer treatment, but the price is a set of immune side effects, and one of them lands on the kidney. To bring some order to it, an international panel from several specialties met at the thirty-fourth Acute Disease Quality Initiative conference in the autumn of two thousand twenty-four, searched the literature systematically, and then argued their way to agreed statements through structured discussion and voting.

(Exact values: 34th ADQI consensus conference, September 2024; modified Delphi with workgroup appraisal and structured voting.)

Start with the epidemiology.

From the observational data the panel weighed up, kidney injury of one kind or another turns up in as many as one patient in five on these drugs, while the immune-related form itself accounts for something like two to five percent of cases.

(Exact values: AKI in up to 20% of patients receiving ICIs; ICI-AKI approximately 2%-5% of cases.)

Then what the biopsies show.

When these kidneys are biopsied, the picture is dominated by acute tubulointerstitial nephritis, which shows up in eight to nine out of every ten samples, though glomerular disease is being seen more often than it used to be. Nothing in the clinical picture separates this reliably from other causes of kidney injury, which is why the panel still treats biopsy as the reference standard.

(Exact values: Acute tubulointerstitial nephritis in 80%-90% of biopsies.)

Then the timing.

Timing appears to matter. Where steroids were begun within three days of the diagnosis rather than later than that, the kidney recovered more often, although how much steroid to give, and for how long, is still an open question.

(Exact values: Glucocorticoids started within 3 days of ICI-AKI diagnosis vs >3 days after diagnosis: associated with higher rates of kidney recovery; optimal dose and duration uncertain.)

And then the question everyone asks next. Can the drug be started again?

When the drug was restarted, the kidney injury came back in fewer than one patient in five, and on that basis the panel supports a cautious rechallenge for selected patients. For the higher-risk groups, kidney transplant recipients, people with autoimmune disease and those with advanced chronic kidney disease, their position is that the decision has to be made case by case, with more than one specialty at the table.

(Exact values: Recurrent ICI-AKI in fewer than 20% of patients undergoing ICI rechallenge.)

One more thing before we move on.

It is worth being clear about what this document is. The statements come from observational data and expert agreement, not from randomised trials. The new biomarkers in urine, blood and imaging look promising but are not ready for everyday use, and the right steroid dose and duration are still unsettled.

Fourth, and we move to The Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology.

This is not a fresh trial but a planned pooling of three of them, done at the level of individual participants. FLOW enrolled people who already had chronic kidney disease, while SELECT and SOUL enrolled people with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and together that came to nearly thirty-one thousand participants. Each of them received semaglutide or a matching placebo on top of whatever care they were already getting, and they were followed for something between three and four years on average.

(Exact values: Pooled participants: N = 30 787 from SELECT, FLOW and SOUL; Mean follow-up 39.5-47.5 months; Semaglutide dosing: 1.0 mg s.c. once weekly (FLOW), 2.4 mg s.c. once weekly (SELECT), 14 mg oral once daily (SOUL).)

So what counted as a kidney event here?

The main endpoint was the time until a first kidney event, and the investigators counted four things under that heading: a lasting drop in filtration rate of half or more, kidney failure, meaning either a filtration rate that stayed below fifteen or the start of kidney replacement therapy, death attributed to the kidneys, and death from cardiovascular causes.

(Exact values: Primary composite (time to first event) — sustained eGFR fall of >=50%; kidney failure, defined as sustained eGFR <15 mL/min per 1.73 m2 or start of kidney replacement therapy; death from kidney causes; death from cardiovascular causes.)

Here is the headline.

Fewer of those kidney events happened on semaglutide than on placebo. In relative terms the risk fell by about sixteen percent, and the confidence interval sat entirely on the side of benefit.

(Exact values: Primary kidney composite, first events: 973 with semaglutide vs 1134 with placebo; hazard ratio 0.84 (95% CI 0.77-0.91).)

Safety next.

On the safety side the two groups looked much alike, and what turned up matched what other trials of this drug class have shown. Serious adverse events were, if anything, a little less common on semaglutide than on placebo.

(Exact values: Safety: comparable across the two groups and consistent with the wider GLP-1 receptor agonist evidence; serious adverse events numerically fewer on semaglutide (event counts not reported in the abstract).)

One more thing to weigh.

The authors put the caveats on the table themselves. The three trials were built along similar lines and tested the same drug, but the people entering them were not alike at baseline, and the dose and the route of administration differed between the trials as well. The funding came from Novo Nordisk.

Last one, and it starts outside our specialty. The Lancet, and an antibiotic trial.

Bloodstream infection with a penicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus was once thought rare and has come back around the world, yet the recommended drugs are still the anti-staphylococcal penicillins, because resistance might go undetected. Inside the SNAP platform trial, adults admitted with this infection at sixty-seven hospitals in eight countries were allocated at random, with everyone aware of what they were getting, to either benzylpenicillin or to flucloxacillin or cloxacillin. The outcome that mattered most was death from any cause ninety days after entering the platform.

(Exact values: Sites: 67 hospitals in 8 countries — Australia, Canada, Israel, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Singapore, South Africa and the UK; 1:1 allocation; adults aged >=18 years reported here; Primary outcome: all-cause mortality 90 days after platform entry, intention-to-treat, all patients with available data; Non-inferiority margin: adjusted odds ratio < 1.20, hierarchical Bayesian logistic regression; interim analyses after every 500 participants reached 90-day follow-up; Dosing: benzylpenicillin 1.8 g IV every 4 h or 2.4 g every 6 h; flucloxacillin 2.0 g IV every 6 h; cloxacillin 2.0 g IV every 4 h; registration NCT05137119.)

Now, how this trial ended.

Recruitment into this part of the platform was halted early, before the planned stopping rules had been met, and the reason was that the safety committee was seeing more acute kidney injury among the patients on flucloxacillin or cloxacillin. By the time it closed, one hundred and fifty-six adults had been allocated to benzylpenicillin and one hundred and twenty-five to the comparator antibiotics.

(Exact values: Randomly assigned: 156 to benzylpenicillin, 125 to flucloxacillin or cloxacillin (281 in total); Recruitment 18 Feb 2022 to 21 Jun 2024; abstract also states 493 adults with PSSA bacteraemia over that window; PSSA silo closed to recruitment 7 Aug 2024; Median age 67 years (IQR 56-77); 87 (31%) female, 194 (69%) male.)

Start with the outcome the trial was built around.

By the ninety-day mark, fourteen percent of the benzylpenicillin patients had died, against twenty-two percent of those given flucloxacillin or cloxacillin. The credible interval around that difference still crossed the line of no effect, so the mortality comparison on its own does not settle the question.

(Exact values: 90-day death: 21/152 (14%) benzylpenicillin vs 26/121 (22%) flucloxacillin or cloxacillin; Adjusted odds ratio 0.67 (95% credible interval 0.35-1.28); Posterior probability of non-inferiority 96.1%; of superiority 88.9%.)

Now the kidney.

Acute kidney injury is where the two antibiotics really parted company. It developed in eleven percent of the hundred and fifty-three patients on benzylpenicillin, compared with twenty-two percent of the hundred and twenty-four who received flucloxacillin or cloxacillin, and this time the credible interval stayed below the line of no effect.

(Exact values: AKI: 17/153 (11%) benzylpenicillin vs 27/124 (22%) flucloxacillin or cloxacillin; Adjusted odds ratio 0.50 (95% credible interval 0.26-0.94); Posterior probability of non-inferiority 99.8%; of superiority 98.4%.)

Before the conclusion, the limits.

There are real caveats here. This was an open trial, so patients and staff alike knew which antibiotic was being used, recruitment stopped before the planned thresholds had been reached, and the formal criterion the investigators had set for calling benzylpenicillin non-inferior was not actually met.

So what do they conclude?

Taking those two results together, the authors land on a clear position: the strong probability that benzylpenicillin is no worse for survival, combined with the lower risk of kidney injury, means it should be the preferred choice over flucloxacillin or cloxacillin when an adult has bacteraemia with a penicillin-susceptible strain.
