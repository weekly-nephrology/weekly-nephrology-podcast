Welcome to Weekly Nephrology AI. This is the episode for August 31, 2026.

This is an educational show about recent nephrology papers, and how far the evidence actually goes.

Today it's Weekly Summary. Five recent clinical studies, from their abstracts.

I'm Emma. I'll tell you what each one found, and which of them are worth your attention.

Before we get into today's episode, let's ease in with a little Small Talk connected to today's topic.

Let's start somewhere small. When you follow protein in the urine, which number do you actually write in the notes?

Most of us answer that without thinking twice. It is worth asking where the habit came from.

There is a long-standing split in how we measure urinary protein. Trials in chronic kidney disease and diabetic kidney disease built their guidelines and risk calculators around the albumin-to-creatinine ratio, while the rare glomerular disease literature, and the treatment algorithms that came out of it, mostly used the protein-to-creatinine ratio.

So which one did you learn? And did anyone ever explain to you why that one and not the other?

Hold on to that question, because it comes back later on.

A quick word on where today's material comes from, because it sets the limits on everything I say.

All five of today's papers are abstract-only. I have read the published abstracts and nothing more, so where an abstract stays quiet, so will I.

What links these five is one thread. Each of them takes something we do out of habit, and asks whether the habit holds up.

Now, let's get started.

First, a randomised trial in JAMA, on sodium bicarbonate during cardiac arrest.

This was a double-blind, placebo-controlled randomised trial run across twenty-one hospitals in Denmark. The patients were adults having a cardiac arrest in hospital who had already received at least one dose of epinephrine, and they were given either sodium bicarbonate or a matching placebo into the vein. The main outcome was sustained return of spontaneous circulation.

(Exact values: 21 hospitals in Denmark; intervention sodium bicarbonate up to 100 mmol, or placebo, intravenously; key secondary outcomes survival at 30 days, and survival at 30 days with modified Rankin Scale score 0 to 3.)

Now, who was actually in the analysis.

The primary analysis included seven hundred and seventy-nine adults who had a cardiac arrest in hospital, split between the bicarbonate arm and the placebo arm. Their median age was seventy-three years, and most of them were men.

(Exact values: randomized 913 patients; primary analysis population 779 patients, 372 sodium bicarbonate and 407 placebo; screened 2913 patients; median age 73 years, IQR 64 to 79; male 502, 64%.)

And the main result.

Among these adults with in-hospital cardiac arrest, sustained return of spontaneous circulation happened in about thirty-nine percent of the bicarbonate group. In the placebo group it was about thirty-seven percent, and that difference was not statistically significant.

(Exact values: sustained ROSC 146/372, 39%, with sodium bicarbonate vs 150/407, 37%, with placebo; risk ratio 1.05, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.24; P = .62.)

The secondary outcomes are where it gets tempting to over-read.

At thirty days, slightly more of these in-hospital cardiac arrest patients were alive in the bicarbonate group than in the placebo group, and the same pattern showed up for survival with a good neurological outcome. In both cases the confidence intervals were wide and included the possibility of no benefit at all, so these secondary results stay uncertain.

(Exact values: alive at 30 days 45/372, 12%, with sodium bicarbonate vs 37/407, 9.1%, with placebo; risk ratio 1.25, 95% CI 0.84 to 1.88. Favorable neurologic outcome at 30 days 30/372, 8.1%, vs 22/407, 5.4%; risk ratio 1.39, 95% CI 0.82 to 2.34.)

Here is the part that lands closest to our own work.

There was a metabolic price, and it is the part nephrologists will recognise. After the arrest, alkalosis and hypernatraemia were both more common in the patients who had received bicarbonate.

And a boundary before we move on.

One boundary is worth drawing. This trial studied adults arresting in hospital who had already received epinephrine, and the abstract reports nothing about specific causes of arrest, so it does not tell us what to do when the arrest is driven by severe hyperkalaemia or profound acidosis.

Now to the opposite end of the day. A quiet outpatient room, and a conversation about food. It comes from the Journal of Renal Nutrition.

This was a randomised crossover trial in sixteen adults with advanced chronic kidney disease, all of them on renin-angiotensin system inhibitors. Each person spent a phase on their usual diet and a phase on a potassium-rich diet built from extra fruit, vegetables and nuts, in random order. Each phase lasted six weeks, and if potassium climbed too high the participant was given sodium zirconium cyclosilicate and stayed on the diet.

(Exact values: 16 patients with advanced CKD receiving RAAS inhibitors; diet phases 6 weeks each, potassium-rich diet adding 40 mmol potassium/day; SZC treatment threshold for hyperkalemia >5.5 mmol/l; primary endpoint serum potassium at 6 weeks, non-inferiority margin 0.5 mmol/l.)

Keep the size of that trial in mind. It shapes everything that follows.

Among the sixteen participants, serum potassium at the end of the diet phases came out almost identical. The between-group difference was only about 0.03 millimoles per litre, well inside the margin the investigators had set in advance.

(Exact values: serum potassium at 6 weeks, between-group difference 0.03 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.32 to 0.27; p = 0.850; pre-specified non-inferiority margin 0.5 mmol/l.)

But did anyone actually eat differently? That is the question a diet trial lives or dies on.

The urine collections confirmed that the diet really did change what people took in. In this trial of adults with advanced chronic kidney disease, potassium excretion rose by roughly twenty-five millimoles a day on the potassium-rich diet.

(Exact values: 24-hour urinary potassium excretion, between-group difference 24.7 mmol/24h, 95% CI 8.0 to 41.3; p = 0.005.)

Now the safety side.

Two of the participants did become hyperkalaemic while on the potassium-rich diet. Both were given the potassium binder, stayed on the diet, and did not become hyperkalaemic again.

(Exact values: hyperkalemia during the potassium-rich diet in 2 patients, potassium 5.9 and 6.1 mmol/L, both treated with SZC with no recurrence.)

And the honest limits of it.

The obvious caveat is size and length. Only sixteen people took part. Each diet phase ran for six weeks, and what was measured was serum potassium rather than any long-term kidney or cardiovascular outcome.

(Exact values: participants 16; duration of each diet phase 6 weeks.)

Next, a change of register. Avacopan in ANCA-associated vasculitis, reported in Clinical and Experimental Nephrology.

This was a look back at records from six hospitals in Kyushu, in Japan, covering adults newly diagnosed with ANCA-associated vasculitis and ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis. The patients given avacopan were the group of interest, and those treated conventionally were used as a reference. What the team followed was death or the need for maintenance kidney replacement therapy, along with remission, complications and drug side effects.

So, who was in it?

Seventy-four adults with newly diagnosed ANCA-associated vasculitis and kidney involvement were included. Twenty of them were treated with avacopan.

(Exact values: patients enrolled 74; patients treated with avacopan 20.)

And how severe was the kidney involvement in that group?

These were not mild cases. The patients who received avacopan had a median eGFR of about fifteen. Median proteinuria was over three grams per gram of creatinine.

(Exact values: median eGFR in avacopan-treated patients 15.4 mL/min/1.73 m2, IQR 9.2 to 38.0; median proteinuria 3.2 g/gCr, IQR 1.2 to 6.3.)

Now the outcomes, and here I want to be careful with my words.

Over two years of follow-up, none of the avacopan-treated patients died. The share who went on to maintenance kidney replacement therapy, and the share who reached complete remission, looked descriptively similar to the conventionally treated patients, but these were unadjusted, side-by-side descriptions rather than a formal comparison.

(Exact values: follow-up 24 months; deaths among the 20 avacopan-treated patients during 24-month follow-up 0; maintenance kidney replacement therapy and complete remission assessed at 6 and 12 months, percentages not given in the abstract.)

Safety is where this cohort gets interesting.

The safety picture pulled in two directions in this cohort. Infections over the two years of follow-up were recorded in a far smaller share of the avacopan-treated patients than of the conventionally treated ones, while raised liver enzymes were the other way round and were reported more often with avacopan.

(Exact values: cumulative incidence of infection over 24 months 16.2% with avacopan vs 50.2% with conventional treatment; liver enzyme elevation 37.2% with avacopan vs 15.5% with conventional treatment.)

And then the caveat that governs all of it.

The limits here are important. This was a retrospective study, the avacopan group was small, and the two treatment groups were simply described side by side rather than compared in a randomised way, so we cannot say that avacopan itself produced the differences that were seen.

Now we come back to the opening question. This paper is in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation.

The team pulled patients from the RaDaR rare kidney disease registry who had both urine measurements taken on the same day. They then built survival models for time to kidney failure or death, adjusted for age, sex, kidney function and underlying disease, and ran them separately with four different urinary protein measures: albumin-to-creatinine ratio, protein-to-creatinine ratio, non-albumin proteinuria, and an albumin-to-creatinine ratio estimated from the protein measurement.

Several measures, the same outcome, the same patients. Here is the scale of it.

The analysis covered more than four thousand people with rare kidney diseases in the registry. Around seventeen percent of them reached kidney failure or died during follow-up.

(Exact values: patients analysed 4156; kidney failure or death events 717, 17%.)

And the headline.

In this registry cohort of rare kidney diseases, all four urinary protein measures predicted kidney failure or death to a similar standard, and none of the differences between them reached statistical significance. Discrimination at five years was high for every model and for every disease subgroup that was tested.

(Exact values: time-dependent AUC at 5 years >0.83 for all models and disease subgroups; model concordance comparisons Chi2 P > .2 for all comparisons; AIC and SBC values very similar across all 4 proteinuria measures, ACR, PCR, non-albumin proteinuria and estACR.)

Though there is a detail here I would actually remember.

There was one practical catch in these patients. When the predicted albumin-to-creatinine ratio sat in the lower part of the range, converting a protein-to-creatinine ratio into an estimated albumin ratio came out too low.

And the limits on what any of that can tell us.

It is worth being clear about what this study was and was not. It is an observational registry analysis of prediction, in people with rare kidney diseases, so it tells us how well each measure tracks the risk of kidney failure or death, not which measure should be used to decide on treatment.

And finally, the paper that sits furthest from the bedside. Also in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation.

Albuminuria is how we normally follow chronic kidney disease and judge whether a treatment is working, but it moves around a lot and it mainly tells us about the glomerulus. This team looked at two urinary markers of the tubules instead: clusterin, which tracks tubular injury, and epidermal growth factor, which tracks tubular repair.

So what happened to those markers while people were on treatment?

In the SONAR population, which was made up of adults with diabetic kidney disease, urinary clusterin fell by roughly half while people were taking atrasentan. Over the same treatment period, serum endothelin-1 went the other way and rose.

(Exact values: uCLU/Cr after 6 weeks of atrasentan -46.3%, 95% CI -57.8 to -37.1; serum ET-1 after 6 weeks of atrasentan +23.4%, 95% CI 19.2 to 27.4; baseline uCLU/Cr vs urinary ET-1 Pearson r = 0.65, P < .0001.)

A different drug, a different marker, a different trial population.

In DAPA-CKD, which included adults with chronic kidney disease from several different causes, dapagliflozin slowed the fall in urinary EGF over the following year. The effect was uneven, though. It was largest in diabetic kidney disease, and it was not seen in glomerulonephritis or hypertensive nephropathy.

(Exact values: uEGF/Cr in DAPA-CKD, decline attenuated by dapagliflozin over 1 year, effect sizes not given in the abstract; larger effects reported in participants with type 2 diabetes and higher baseline HbA1c.)

Before anyone gets excited, here is the ceiling on all of this.

One thing to keep in mind is that this work stops at the biomarkers. The abstract tells us how the markers moved on treatment, not whether those movements translate into fewer people losing kidney function, and it does not tell us how many participants were analysed.

A handful of things I would carry out of today. Start with the clearest.

The authors' bottom line is blunt. These findings do not support giving sodium bicarbonate routinely to adults having a cardiac arrest in hospital.

Next, the food question.

The authors' conclusion is a careful one. They suggest that in selected patients with advanced chronic kidney disease a potassium-rich diet may be workable, as long as potassium is watched closely and a binder is available when it is needed.

And on measurement, where the closing papers spent their time.

The authors' reading is that these urine markers report on different intrarenal pathways, one for each drug class, and that they add something to albuminuria by showing what is happening in the tubules.
