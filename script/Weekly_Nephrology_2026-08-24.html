Blood in the urine is one of the hallmarks of IgA nephropathy and largely reflects inflammation in the glomeruli, yet risk assessment in current guidelines leans mainly on proteinuria and estimated GFR.

We open in Clinical Kidney Journal, with a treatment trial in IgA nephropathy.

Sibeprenlimab is being tested in people with IgA nephropathy in a global phase three trial called VISIONARY, and this report looks specifically at the patients enrolled in mainland China.

So how was it set up? This matters more than it sounds, because who gets counted at an interim look is not the same group as who was enrolled.

In the China cohort, one hundred and two patients with IgA nephropathy were randomly assigned to sibeprenlimab or placebo, given as an injection under the skin every four weeks, and by the interim cutoff forty-two of them had reached the nine-month proteinuria assessment.

(Exact values: China cohort randomized: N = 102, 1:1 sibeprenlimab 400 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks for 26 doses vs placebo; Interim efficacy analysis: 42 patients (sibeprenlimab n = 25, placebo n = 17); China interim data cutoff: January 2025.)

Here's the headline number.

At nine months, twenty-four-hour urine protein had fallen by roughly sixty per cent more in the sibeprenlimab group than in the placebo group, where the level barely moved.

(Exact values: Sibeprenlimab: 63.1% reduction in 24-hour uPCR from baseline at 9 months; Placebo: 3.1% decrease from baseline; Placebo-adjusted reduction 61.9% (95% CI 43.2% to 74.5%).)

Keep the timing in mind, though. A number that size, at a single point in a trial, is a snapshot rather than a destination.

There's more in the same analysis, and it goes beyond the urine.

Spot urine protein started dropping by week four and stayed down through month twelve, the disease-related biomarkers galactose-deficient IgA-one and APRIL both fell substantially by week forty-eight, and treatment-emergent adverse events were similar in the two groups.

(Exact values: Gd-IgA1 reduced 67.3% from baseline at week 48; APRIL suppressed 97.7% from baseline at week 48; Spot uPCR declined by week 4 and was sustained through month 12.)

Here is how the authors themselves read it.

The authors describe the China findings as being in line with what the global trial population showed at the same interim stage.

Now the part that decides how much weight any of this can carry.

This is an interim look at a small subgroup of the trial, so the number of patients analysed is small, and the key kidney function endpoint, the eGFR slope over twenty-four months, will only be reported when the whole trial finishes.

(Exact values: Interim efficacy analysis: 42 of 102 patients randomized in the China cohort; Key secondary endpoint: annualized eGFR slope over 24 months, to be reported at trial completion.)

Which brings us back to that dipstick from a moment ago. This one is in Kidney International.

The researchers brought together four hundred and forty-one patients with biopsy-proven IgA nephropathy from several centres and compared the urine findings taken around the time of biopsy with the Oxford classification lesions, using unadjusted models that were then pooled across the centres.

(Exact values: 441 patients with biopsy-proven IgA nephropathy; Biopsies from July 2015 to July 2025; Analysis: unadjusted logistic regression in each of the contributing cohorts, pooled into 1 random-effects odds ratio per lesion.)

So what do the urine findings line up with under the microscope? And which of the urine markers carries the most information?

In these patients with IgA nephropathy, dipstick hemoglobinuria was associated with the active lesions on biopsy — mesangial hypercellularity, endocapillary hypercellularity and crescents — each with roughly sixty to eighty per cent higher odds.

(Exact values: Hemoglobinuria and M1 lesions: pooled OR 1.77 (95% CI 1.25-2.51); Hemoglobinuria and E1 lesions: OR 1.75 (95% CI 1.44-2.11); Hemoglobinuria and crescentic lesions: OR 1.57 (95% CI 1.20-2.07).)

Here is the comparison I found most interesting.

For the combined marker of active inflammatory lesions, dipstick hemoglobinuria showed the strongest association, more than doubling the odds, while microscopic hematuria and proteinuria showed weaker associations.

(Exact values: Composite of M1, E1 and/or C1/C2 — hemoglobinuria: pooled OR 2.28 (95% CI 1.76-2.97); microscopic hematuria: OR 1.36 (95% CI 1.22-1.51); proteinuria: OR 1.20 (95% CI 1.04-1.38).)

Then there is the flip side, which tells you just as much.

The urine blood markers were not linked with chronic scarring lesions, whereas proteinuria was, and the authors suggest that a simple dipstick reading for blood could add information about how active IgA nephropathy is.

(Exact values: Proteinuria and T1/T2 lesions: pooled OR 1.35 (95% CI 1.20-1.52); Hemoglobinuria and microscopic hematuria vs chronic lesions: no association reported (441 patients).)

Before we leave it, the limits.

Because this was a retrospective study using unadjusted models, the findings describe associations at the time of biopsy, and they cannot tell us how these patients went on to do.

Third study, and a change of scene. We move from the biopsy report to the kitchen table, and to the journal Kidney Three Sixty.

High phosphate is a frequent metabolic problem in chronic kidney disease, and managing it through diet is hard, because the diet itself is complicated and dietitian time is limited.

If you work in a dialysis unit, none of that will be news to you. It is the daily grind of the job.

So somebody tried to help with that, and then did the harder thing. They tested it properly.

In this six-month randomised trial across twenty-three Australian dialysis units, one hundred and ninety patients on dialysis took part, with ninety-nine of them assigned to add a dietary smartphone app to usual care for the first three months, and the trial's main measure was how far serum phosphate moved by month three.

(Exact values: 190 dialysis patients recruited; 99 randomized to the intervention; 23 sites in Australia; six-month trial with the app provided for the first 3 months; Primary outcome: between-group change in serum phosphate from baseline to 3 months.)

And the primary result.

The primary result was negative: there was no significant difference between the app group and usual care in how much serum phosphate changed by three months, and both groups fell by only a few per cent.

(Exact values: Reduction in serum phosphate at month 3: control 6%, intervention 7%; Primary outcome at 3 months: no significant between-group difference in change in serum phosphate.)

A negative primary outcome is not a failed study. It is an answer, and it deserves the same attention as a positive one.

The story doesn't stop at month three, though.

Only the app group kept improving after the app period ended, reaching about a nine per cent fall in phosphate by month six, and even that did not reach statistical significance.

(Exact values: Intervention group: 9% reduction in serum phosphate at month 6, not statistically significant.)

There is a subgroup finding too, and I want to be careful with it.

In an exploratory post-hoc analysis, women — especially those aged sixty-five and over — had the biggest falls in phosphate, around twenty per cent by month six.

(Exact values: Women, particularly aged 65 years or older: -0.93 mg/dL (13%) at month 3 and -1.36 mg/dL (20%) at month 6.)

Careful how? Like this.

Because the main comparison was negative and the later findings come from within-group and exploratory analyses, these extra results are hypothesis-generating rather than proof that the app lowers phosphate.

Fourth, and this one answers a question you have probably had at the bedside. It's in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation.

Monoclonal gammopathy becomes common as people get older and may influence autoimmune disease, but what it means for patients with ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis has hardly been evaluated.

It is the kind of incidental result that lands on you in the clinic room, with the patient still sitting in front of you.

Here is how they went looking.

This was a multicentre retrospective study of three hundred and thirty-two patients with ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis from seven centres, with a median age of sixty-five, in whom a monoclonal protein was looked for at the time of diagnosis.

(Exact values: 332 patients with ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis from 7 centers; Median age 65 years (IQR 56-75); 59% male; Monoclonal gammopathy ascertained at diagnosis in all 332 patients by serum electrophoresis and/or immunofixation.)

First, how often it turned up.

A monoclonal protein was found in about fifteen per cent of these patients with ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis; they tended to be older and more often male, with more hypertension and lower albumin, while their kidney function, disease activity score, ANCA subtype and biopsy findings were similar.

(Exact values: Monoclonal gammopathy detected in 50 of 332 patients (15.1%).)

Then what happened to those patients over the years that followed.

Over a median follow-up of nearly five years, patients with a monoclonal protein had worse overall survival, more severe infections and more cancers in the unadjusted analyses, with roughly double the risk of dying.

(Exact values: Median follow-up 57 months; Overall survival: HR 2.19 (95% CI 1.28-3.75), p = 0.004; Severe infection: HR 1.76 (95% CI 1.17-2.64), p = 0.007; Cancer: HR 2.07 (95% CI 1.08-3.95), p = 0.028.)

Now, before you carry any of that forward, watch what happens next.

After adjustment, the monoclonal protein no longer independently predicted death, infection or cancer, it was not linked with progression to kidney failure, relapse or cardiovascular events, and no patient went on to develop myeloma or a related blood cancer during follow-up.

So how much should that finding move you? The authors are cautious, and I think rightly.

Since this was a retrospective study and only a minority of patients had a monoclonal protein, the authors read the association as mostly reflecting older age and coexisting illness rather than a disease-modifying effect, and residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

Last one, and it's in Cardiorenal Medicine.

SGLT2 inhibitors have well established long-term heart and kidney benefits in type two diabetes, but whether a short course around a contrast scan prevents contrast-induced acute kidney injury has been uncertain.

It is a tempting idea. And tempting ideas are exactly the ones that need a proper control arm before anyone believes them.

So they went at that question directly.

In this single-centre, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, diabetic patients booked for a coronary CT angiogram were randomly assigned to dapagliflozin ten milligrams daily or placebo, starting three days before the scan and continuing for seventy-two hours after it, and three hundred and twenty-two patients with complete creatinine data made up the main analysis, one hundred and sixty-one in each group.

(Exact values: 352 screened, 335 randomized, 322 in the modified intention-to-treat analysis (161 per group); Dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily from 3 days before CCTA through the 72-hour follow-up; CI-AKI defined by prespecified serum creatinine criteria within 72 hours of contrast exposure.)

And the primary outcome.

Contrast-induced acute kidney injury occurred in about nine per cent of these diabetic patients overall, and it was clearly less frequent with dapagliflozin than with placebo — under four per cent against roughly fourteen per cent.

(Exact values: CI-AKI overall: 29 of 322 patients (9.0%); Dapagliflozin 6/161 (3.7%) vs placebo 23/161 (14.3%); Odds ratio 0.23 (95% CI 0.09-0.59), P = 0.001 by Fisher exact test.)

Hold that thought about how firm this is. First, what else they measured.

Baseline kidney measures were similar in the two groups, and at seventy-two hours the dapagliflozin group had lower creatinine and higher eGFR, with lower urea and NGAL in exploratory analyses, although the spread of AKI stages did not differ significantly and no patient needed new dialysis during the follow-up.

(Exact values: KDIGO creatinine-based AKI stage distribution: P = 0.064; New dialysis or kidney replacement therapy: 0 patients through 72 hours.)

Here is how the authors put it.

The authors read this as a signal that a short course of dapagliflozin around the scan is linked with fewer creatinine-defined episodes of contrast-induced kidney injury in diabetic patients, while stressing that larger multicentre trials are needed before anything changes.

And now the caution I promised you.

This was a single-centre trial with very few events and only seventy-two hours of follow-up, and the outcome was based on creatinine alone, so the statistical models had to be kept deliberately simple.

(Exact values: Single-center trial; 29 CI-AKI events among 322 analysed patients; 72-hour follow-up.)
