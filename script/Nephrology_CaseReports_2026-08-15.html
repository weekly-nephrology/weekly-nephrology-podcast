Now to our deep-read case. This is the one where we have the whole paper, not a summary.

It is a report in CEN Case Reports, published this August. We will take it slowly.

Lysozyme is an enzyme that monocytes and their tumour counterparts pour into the blood. It slips easily through the glomerulus, and the proximal tubule takes it back up. When more arrives than those tubular cells can handle, the cells are injured, and that injury is what the authors call lysozyme-induced nephropathy. It shows up mainly with chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia, but it has also been reported with myelodysplastic syndrome.

So the tubule is not being invaded. It is asked to take back more than it can handle, and it fails.

That urine is telling us something specific. The problem sits below the filter, in the plumbing rather than the sieve.

The second patient was another Japanese man in his eighties, already living with chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia, whose creatinine had been climbing for months. So much for what happened. Now to what the authors make of it.

Putting the two men side by side is where it gets interesting. The kidney findings looked essentially the same even though the underlying blood disorders were different. And in the myelodysplastic syndrome case there was no sustained rise in monocytes in the blood, yet the marrow was full of lysozyme-positive cells. The authors read that as a hint that marrow cells making lysozyme, not only circulating monocytes, may be enough to injure the tubules.

(Exact values: Case 1 bone marrow: numerous lysozyme-positive cells despite a myeloblast ratio of 0.8% and no sustained absolute monocytosis; Cited literature in the discussion: myelodysplastic syndrome accounted for 14% of lysozyme-induced nephropathy cases in the referenced multicentre report; Proteinuria reported in 77% of lysozyme-induced nephropathy cases in the same referenced report.)

So the marrow, not only the monocytes in the blood, may be where the lysozyme comes from. That is their reading, held loosely.
