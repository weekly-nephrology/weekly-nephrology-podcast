The paper is in the Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, out this September, from the University Medical Center Groningen in the Netherlands.

The MIRO-CKD phase two trial had already shown that adding the non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist balcinrenone to dapagliflozin lowers albuminuria in chronic kidney disease, while also producing a small early drop in filtration rate. This prespecified analysis set out to work out what that early dip actually means.

Three hundred twenty-four adults with chronic kidney disease, all already on renin-angiotensin blockade, were randomly assigned either to one of two balcinrenone plus dapagliflozin doses or to dapagliflozin alone. Filtration rate was checked four weeks after randomisation and again four weeks after treatment was stopped.

(Exact values: n=324 adults randomized; eGFR >=25 to <60 mL/min/1.73 m2; UACR >=100 to <5000 mg/g.)

So what did the filtration rate actually do in those first weeks?

Among these adults with chronic kidney disease, the early fall in filtration rate compared with dapagliflozin alone was well under one millilitre per minute at the lower balcinrenone dose and under two millilitres per minute at the higher dose. In other words, the dip was modest at both doses.

(Exact values: balcinrenone/dapagliflozin 15/10 mg vs dapagliflozin/placebo at Week 4: -0.6 mL/min/1.73 m2, 95% CI -2.4 to 1.1; 40/10 mg: -1.7 mL/min/1.73 m2, 95% CI -3.5 to -0.01.)

In the same trial population, the early reduction in filtration rate came back up once treatment had been off for four weeks, and the pattern looked the same across the subgroups the investigators examined.

Reversible is the word to hold on to there.

Among these adults with chronic kidney disease, the participants whose filtration rate dropped most early on were also the ones whose albuminuria and systolic blood pressure fell most. Serious adverse events stayed uncommon, were similar between the arms, and did not track with the size of the early dip.

This was an exploratory analysis of a short phase two trial whose main endpoint was albuminuria, so it tells us how filtration rate behaves in the first weeks rather than what happens to kidneys over years. The comparison between people with bigger and smaller dips is also observational, not randomised.

(Exact values: phase 2 trial with a 12-week UACR primary outcome.)

Whether one early measurement should carry weight on its own is a question that comes back more than once today.

Which brings us from a trial population to the clinic that trials rarely look like.

This one is in the same journal, published in August, from the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm.

Swedish investigators used the national renal registry to follow adults with chronic kidney disease who were under specialist nephrology care, linking them to national pharmacy records to see who started an SGLT2 inhibitor. They used weighting methods designed to handle the fact that treatment decisions change over time.

The cohort held nearly thirty-three thousand adults with chronic kidney disease, median age seventy-three, with a mean filtration rate of about twenty-nine millilitres per minute, so this was an advanced group. Roughly one in seven were ever exposed to an SGLT2 inhibitor.

(Exact values: 32,856 participants; median age 73 years, IQR 63-79; 36% women; mean eGFR 29.3 +/- 16.8 ml/min per 1.73 m2; 4984 ever-exposed, 15.2%.)

Now the outcomes.

Among these nephrology-managed patients with chronic kidney disease, taking an SGLT2 inhibitor was associated with about a twenty percent lower risk of major cardiovascular events and roughly a third lower risk of needing kidney replacement therapy.

(Exact values: MACE odds ratio 0.80, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.91; KRT odds ratio 0.67, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.85.)

In the same cohort, overall mortality and the combined heart failure or cardiovascular death outcome were not clearly different, although the analysis limited to brand-new users did suggest a lower risk of that heart failure outcome.

(Exact values: all-cause mortality odds ratio 0.95, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.15; heart failure hospitalization or CV death odds ratio 0.87, 95% CI 0.65 to 1.16; incident-user analysis 0.61, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.98.)

The reason to spend time on a registry study is the people in it, so it's worth asking who they were.

The associations held up across the prespecified subgroups, including people with advanced kidney disease who are thin on the ground in the randomised trials. The authors read that as evidence that the trial findings travel reasonably well into everyday nephrology practice.

And then the caveat, which is structural rather than cosmetic.

This is a registry cohort rather than a trial, so even with the weighting methods the numbers are associations. Doctors choose who gets these drugs, and that choice can leave traces the analysis cannot fully remove.

A synopsis in Annals of Internal Medicine this September, written by nephrologists at Stony Brook University and at UT Southwestern in Dallas.

KDIGO has published its twenty twenty-six guideline on anaemia in chronic kidney disease, the first full update since twenty twelve. It covers how anaemia is diagnosed and then iron, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, the HIF prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors, and transfusion.

An international work group set the scope, an independent team ran the systematic reviews up to October of twenty twenty-four and graded the certainty of the evidence, and stakeholders reviewed the draft. The final document carries eight graded recommendations plus forty-nine practice points.

(Exact values: systematic reviews through October 2024; 8 graded recommendations; 49 practice points.)

Iron first.

For iron, the guideline anchors the decision on transferrin saturation and ferritin thresholds, and in people on haemodialysis it prefers the intravenous route over oral iron.

Then to the drugs themselves.

Before reaching for an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent or a HIF prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor, the guideline asks that correctable causes of anaemia be dealt with first. When a drug is needed, it puts the ESA ahead of the HIF-PHI as first-line treatment.

The threshold for starting an ESA is individualised to the patient's characteristics, symptoms and their own values, and during maintenance the guideline sets an upper haemoglobin ceiling of eleven point five grams per decilitre.

(Exact values: upper haemoglobin target below 11.5 g/dL, that is below 115 g/L, during ESA maintenance therapy.)

There's one more feature of the document that's easy to miss, and it changes how you read the rest of it.

Alongside the eight graded recommendations sit forty-nine practice points, and the guideline is explicit that these reflect expert consensus where the evidence was thin or a systematic review could not be done. They are not on the same footing as the graded statements.

Now a question about a pattern many of us have been taught to treat gently.

It's a multicentre cohort in Clinical Kidney Journal, this September, led from the Humanitas Research Hospital in Milan.

Pure membranous lupus nephritis, class five on its own, has long carried a reputation as the gentler form compared with the mixed lesions that also have proliferative changes. The authors point out that only two trials have compared the two, and neither settled the question.

This was a retrospective multicentre study of one hundred ninety-five patients whose first biopsy showed either the pure or the mixed form, split almost evenly between the two. The team tracked response at one year, sustained response, relapses, and survival free of kidney failure or death.

(Exact values: 195 patients; 96 pure membranous lupus nephritis, 49.2%; 99 mixed, 50.8%.)

At the time of biopsy, the pure membranous patients looked milder on immunological grounds and had lower activity and chronicity scores on histology. They also went on to get lighter immunosuppression, with less intravenous steroid and earlier withdrawal of treatment.

(Exact values: NIH activity and chronicity indices lower in pure membranous lupus nephritis, P < .001.)

Here's where it gets uncomfortable.

Among these lupus nephritis patients, responses, relapses and kidney survival came out similar in the two groups, but survival free of kidney failure or death was actually worse in the pure membranous patients. That difference persisted after adjusting for baseline kidney function and for the induction regimen they received.

(Exact values: kidney failure or death-free survival lower in pure membranous lupus nephritis, P = .026; adjusted Cox model, mixed disease had an 80% lower risk of kidney failure or death, P = .0384.)

Before you read into that, listen to what the authors themselves put next to it.

One caveat comes straight from the paper: when they split by era, the crude survival gap between the two forms was only confirmed in patients biopsied before the year two thousand.

Because treatment was chosen by clinicians rather than randomised, the idea that lighter immunosuppression drove the worse outcome stays a hypothesis. The fact that the difference was confirmed only in the older biopsy era adds to that uncertainty.

Which takes us back to the number we opened with, and to the study behind it.

Also in Clinical Kidney Journal this September, from the Mario Negri Institute in Bergamo.

Fifty patients with primary membranous nephropathy and heavy proteinuria had their filtration rate measured directly by iohexol clearance, twice, a year apart. Six estimating equations based on creatinine, cystatin C, or both were then judged against those direct measurements.

(Exact values: 50 patients, 100 blood samples collected 1 year apart; proteinuria >3.5 g/24 h; 6 equations: CKD-EPI and EKFC, each creatinine-based, cystatin C-based and combined.)

In these nephrotic patients the two marker families pulled in opposite directions: creatinine-based estimates read higher than the measured filtration rate, while cystatin C-based estimates read lower, and that was true for both equation families.

Which raises the obvious question about using the two markers together.

In these nephrotic patients the combined creatinine and cystatin C equation was biased whenever albumin was low, and the bias grew as the albumin fell, regardless of how much protein was being lost. Only in those with normal albumin did the combined equations reach acceptable agreement, and even there the error was over ten percent in more than four estimates out of ten.

(Exact values: eGFRcr+cys bias significant in hypoalbuminemic participants, P < 0.05; in normoalbuminemic participants CKD-EPI and EKFC eGFRcr+cys concordance correlation coefficient not different from the 0.90 reference, total deviation index <30; error exceeded 10% in >40% of estimates.)

The authors conclude that in a nephrotic patient the equations are only acceptable when creatinine and cystatin C are combined and the albumin is normal. When albumin is low, or when a decision such as dosing a toxic renally cleared drug rests on the number, they say filtration should be measured directly.

It is worth remembering the scale: fifty patients, all with primary membranous nephropathy, all taking part in intervention studies. Whether the same size of error applies to other causes of nephrotic syndrome was not tested here.

The sixth paper sits furthest from the clinic today, so I'll keep it short.

It's in Kidney International, this September, from the Charite in Berlin with colleagues at Yale.

In both ANCA-associated vasculitis and lupus nephritis, what happens in the tubules and the interstitium, rather than in the glomerulus alone, does a lot to decide the long-term prognosis. Working out which immune cells injure those resident cells could point towards treatment targets.

The team ran imaging mass cytometry on kidney biopsies from five patients with ANCA-associated vasculitis, five with proliferative lupus nephritis, and five reference samples, then clustered more than a hundred thousand individual cells without preconceptions about what they would find.

(Exact values: 15 patients, 5 AAV, 5 class III and IV lupus nephritis, 5 reference; 106,455 cells clustered.)

Against the reference tissue, resident cells in both the vasculitis and the lupus nephritis biopsies had lost some of their differentiation markers and had turned up two injury-related proteins, phosphorylated RIPK3 and the fatty acid ligase FACL4, in several tubule segments and in endothelial cells.

The spatial picture was the interesting part. In these vasculitis and lupus nephritis biopsies, the injury markers were highest in resident cells sitting immediately next to infiltrating immune cells, and T cells above all.

The authors call this hypothesis-generating, and with only five patients in each group that is fair. The comparison tissue came from non-tumour parts of removed kidneys, and no clinical outcomes or treatment responses were reported.

Last one, and it's the hardest kind of trial to talk about.

It's in ESC Heart Failure, published in August, run from the Hospices Civils de Lyon in France.

Type two cardiorenal syndrome is the situation where chronic heart failure drags the kidneys down with it, and it carries a heavy burden of illness and death. When congestion will not shift despite maximal diuretics, mechanical fluid removal sometimes gets proposed.

UF-CARE was an open-label randomised trial run at fifteen French centres. Of one hundred eight patients screened, forty-six were randomised, twenty-four to optimised medical therapy alone and twenty-two to that therapy plus ultrafiltration, given as peritoneal dialysis, haemodialysis or isolated ultrafiltration depending on the patient and the centre.

(Exact values: 15 French centres; 108 screened, 46 randomized, 24 control and 22 ultrafiltration; primary outcome all-cause mortality or unplanned hospitalization for acute heart failure within 12 months.)

Among these patients with severe heart failure and kidney disease, after a median follow-up of around nine months, the composite of death or an unplanned heart failure admission had happened in roughly two thirds of the ultrafiltration group and in getting on for nine in ten of the control group. That gap did not reach statistical significance.

(Exact values: median follow-up 262 days; primary outcome 63% ultrafiltration versus 87% control, P = .144.)

Quality-of-life scores seemed to improve over time in both arms, with the improvement holding a little better on ultrafiltration. The trial also reported one death related to the technique itself.

Only forty-six of the one hundred eight screened patients ended up randomised, and the authors say plainly that the trial was underpowered. So a neutral result here does not settle whether ultrafiltration helps.

The trial was open-label, and the choice between peritoneal dialysis, haemodialysis and isolated ultrafiltration was left to the centre and the patient. That makes this a comparison of an overall strategy against medical therapy, not a head-to-head of the techniques.

Which is the honest place to leave it.
